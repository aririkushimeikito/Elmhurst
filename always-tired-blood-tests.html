Always Tired? These Are the Blood Tests to Ask For

Why am I always tired? An Elmhurst, Queens doctor explains the blood tests worth asking for — CBC, ferritin, TSH, A1C, vitamin D, B12 — and the red flags.

By Dr. Ferdous Salauddin, MD, Internal Medicine, Elmhurst Medical · Medically reviewed by Keerthana Rajagopal, PA · Updated August 2026

You sleep, and you wake up tired anyway. You get through the day on coffee and willpower. And every time you try to explain it, someone says it's just stress — or your age, or the kids, or the commute.

Maybe it is. But "why am I always tired" is one of the most common questions people bring to a primary care office, and it deserves a real answer, not a shrug. Fatigue is among the top 10 reasons people come to primary care and is reported by roughly 5% to 10% of primary care patients, according to a review in American Family Physician. For a lot of them, a short list of blood tests answers the question.

What's normal tiredness, and what isn't?

Normal tiredness has a cause you can name and a fix that works. You were up late, you worked a double, you have a newborn — and after real rest, you bounce back.

The kind worth investigating looks different:

  • Rest doesn't fix it. You sleep 8 hours and still feel wrung out.
  • It's changed. You used to climb the subway stairs without thinking; now you stop halfway.
  • It comes with something else — shortness of breath, weight change, hair loss, numb hands, heavy periods, snoring.
  • It's lasted a while. Six months or more counts as chronic fatigue.

Start with sleep, honestly. The CDC recommends 7 or more hours a night for adults ages 18–60, and depending on the state, 30% to 46% of US adults get less. If you're sleeping five hours, no blood test will explain your fatigue. If you're sleeping seven or eight and still exhausted, keep reading.

Which blood tests actually matter for fatigue?

There's no single "tired panel," and ordering every test in the catalog isn't smart medicine — AAFP notes that lab testing without a specific reason changes treatment in only about 5% of patients. These are the tests a careful history usually points toward.

Complete blood count (CBC) — looking for anemia

A CBC counts your red cells, white cells, and platelets. Low hemoglobin means anemia, which causes fatigue, pallor, shortness of breath, and trouble concentrating, per the NIDDK.

Ferritin and iron studies — low iron even when the CBC is normal

This is the test people most often have to ask for. Ferritin measures stored iron, and you can be iron deficient with a completely normal CBC. The World Health Organization defines low ferritin in adults as under 15 µg/L, but in everyday practice a level under 30 µg/L is treated as iron deficiency — and fatigue can start before hemoglobin ever drops. Ferritin rises with inflammation, so the threshold goes up in that setting: under 100 µg/L in chronic inflammation, and the WHO suggests under 70 µg/L in adults with infection or inflammation. A plain "serum iron" level swings through the day and shouldn't be used alone.

TSH — your thyroid

An underactive thyroid causes fatigue, weight gain, cold intolerance, joint and muscle pain, dry skin and thinning hair, heavy or irregular periods, and depression. Nearly 5 out of 100 Americans age 12 and older have hypothyroidism, and women and people over 60 are at higher risk.

A1C and glucose — diabetes and prediabetes

The American Diabetes Association sets prediabetes at an A1C of 5.7%–6.4% and diabetes at 6.5% or higher; by fasting glucose, prediabetes is 100–125 mg/dL and diabetes 126 mg/dL or above. This matters a lot for our patients: South Asians have at least a two-fold higher prevalence of type 2 diabetes than non-Hispanic whites, and in the MASALA study 23% of South Asian participants had diabetes, versus 6% of non-Hispanic whites and 17% of Latino participants.

Vitamin D

The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements considers a 25(OH)D level below 12 ng/mL deficient, 12 to under 20 ng/mL inadequate, and 20 ng/mL or more sufficient for most people. Low levels can cause muscle weakness and pain.

Two groups we see constantly are at real risk. Melanin absorbs the UVB light your skin needs to make vitamin D, so people with darker skin make less of it — in NHANES 2001–2010, deficiency among people not taking supplements was 75% in non-Hispanic Black adults and 44% in Hispanic adults, versus 20% in non-Hispanic white adults. The NIH also lists people who wear long robes, long dresses, or head coverings for religious reasons among those unlikely to get enough from sunlight. If that's you, ask for this test by name.

Vitamin B12

B12 deficiency causes fatigue, palpitations, a sore tongue, pale skin, and numbness or tingling in the hands and feet. Per the NIH, nerve symptoms can appear without anemia, and untreated nerve damage can become permanent — so catching it early matters. Most labs call values below 200–250 pg/mL subnormal; in the gray zone of 150–399 pg/mL, a methylmalonic acid (MMA) level helps confirm it.

B12 occurs naturally only in animal foods, so long-term vegetarians and vegans are at higher risk — relevant for the many families in Jackson Heights who have eaten vegetarian for decades. Metformin, a common diabetes medication, can also reduce B12 absorption.

Kidney and liver function

A comprehensive metabolic panel checks kidney function, liver enzymes, electrolytes, and calcium. It's part of the basic fatigue workup in AAFP's suggested approach, along with CBC, TSH, ESR, and an HIV test. More than 1 in 7 people with kidney disease have anemia — one way kidney problems cause fatigue.

Two things a blood test won't catch: sleep apnea and depression

Obstructive sleep apnea causes snoring, gasping in your sleep, daytime sleepiness, and trouble with concentration and memory. It's diagnosed with a sleep study, not bloodwork. Depression and anxiety are also common causes of fatigue, and the US Preventive Services Task Force recommends screening all adults for depression (Grade B). The usual tools are the PHQ-9, the GAD-7, and the STOP-Bang questionnaire for sleep apnea risk.

When to see a doctor about this

Get evaluated promptly — not in a few months — if your fatigue comes with any of these red flags, which raise suspicion for serious disease according to a review of fatigue guidelines:

  • Unintentional weight loss you can't explain
  • Fever or night sweats that keep coming back
  • Abnormal bleeding — blood in stool, black stools, coughing blood, unusually heavy periods
  • Shortness of breath at rest or with less activity than before
  • Swollen glands that don't go away
  • New fatigue in an older adult who was previously well

Call 911 or go to an emergency room for chest pain that won't go away or serious difficulty breathing — the NIDDK is explicit about this. Fainting, a racing or irregular heartbeat, or confusion are emergencies too, not next-week problems.

Frequently asked questions

What blood tests should I ask for if I'm always tired?+

A reasonable starting set is CBC, ferritin, TSH, A1C, a metabolic panel, vitamin D, and B12, plus screening questions for sleep apnea and depression. Your provider may add or subtract based on your history — testing without a reason rarely changes treatment.

Can I be low in iron if my blood count is normal?+

Yes. That's iron deficiency without anemia, and it can cause fatigue and exercise intolerance while hemoglobin stays normal. It's why ferritin, not just a CBC, belongs in the workup.

Could my tiredness just be depression?+

It could be part of it — and that's not a lesser explanation. Fatigue is one of the nine PHQ-9 items, and depression screening is recommended for all adults. Many people have both a medical cause and a mood cause, and both deserve treatment; more on that in talking to your primary care doctor about mental health.

Should men get anything extra checked?+

Fatigue overlaps with several conditions covered in the health checks every man over 30 should get, including blood pressure, lipids, and diabetes screening.
