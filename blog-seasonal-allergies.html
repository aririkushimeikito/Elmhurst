Seasonal Allergies Are Getting Worse: 2025-2026 Relief

If your sneezing, stuffy nose, and itchy eyes feel worse than they used to, it is not just you. Allergy seasons are starting earlier and lasting longer — and there are simple, proven ways to feel better.

Every spring and fall, the same thing happens across Elmhurst and Queens. The trees, grasses, and weeds release tiny grains called pollen into the air. For millions of people, breathing that pollen sets off sneezing, a runny nose, and itchy, watery eyes. Doctors call this allergic rhinitis. Most people call it hay fever.

Here is what many of our patients notice: it seems worse every year. That feeling is real. Allergy seasons are starting earlier, lasting longer, and packing more pollen into the air than they did a generation ago. The good news is that you do not have to just suffer through it. This guide explains why seasons are changing and, more importantly, the simple steps that bring real relief.

Why allergy seasons keep getting worse

Allergies are common — and getting more common. In 2024, about 1 in 4 U.S. adults (25.2%) had diagnosed seasonal allergies, along with roughly 1 in 5 children (20.6%), according to new data from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). All told, more than 100 million Americans live with allergies or asthma, reports the Asthma and Allergy Foundation of America (AAFA).

The main reason the seasons feel harder is a warming climate. When the weather stays warm later into fall and heats up earlier in spring, plants have more time to grow and release pollen. Climate Central, a group of scientists who study climate, looked at 198 U.S. cities and found that 173 of them — about 87% — now have a longer growing season than in 1970. On average, the frost-free growing season is 21 days longer than it was in 1970. A longer growing season means a longer allergy season.

~20 days — how much longer North American pollen seasons became between 1990 and 2018, with human-caused warming as the main driver, according to Climate Central. The same research warns pollen amounts could rise sharply as carbon dioxide levels climb.

It is not only about more days. Warmer air and higher carbon dioxide levels can push plants to make more pollen, and stronger pollen. That is why AAFA's yearly Allergy Capitals report now points to climate change as a driving force behind harder allergy seasons. In 2026, extreme weather in the West — heavy storms and rain that fed fast plant growth — helped push cities like Boise, Idaho to the top of the list. AAFA's president, Kenneth Mendez, called the trend a public health problem fueled by climate change.

The takeaway is not to panic. It is to plan ahead — because the season now arrives sooner and stays longer than you may expect.

Do you have hay fever? Know the symptoms

Allergic rhinitis (hay fever) happens when your immune system treats harmless pollen as a threat and fights it. That reaction causes the symptoms you feel. According to the American College of Allergy, Asthma and Immunology (ACAAI), common signs include:

  • Sneezing, often many times in a row
  • A runny nose, or a stuffy, blocked nose
  • Itchy, red, or watery eyes
  • An itchy nose, mouth, or throat
  • A scratchy or sore throat, sometimes from post-nasal drip
  • Feeling tired and worn out — often because a stuffy nose ruins your sleep

One thing to know: allergies do not cause a fever, and they do not spread from person to person. If your "cold" comes back at the same time every year, lasts for weeks, and comes with itchy eyes, allergies are the more likely cause. A cold usually clears in a week to ten days; hay fever can last as long as the pollen is in the air.

How to get real relief

You can do a lot to feel better, and most of it is simple and low-cost. Think of relief in three parts: lower your exposure, treat early, and find your triggers.

1. Lower your exposure to pollen. You cannot avoid pollen completely, but you can cut how much reaches you. ACAAI suggests keeping windows closed and using air conditioning at home and in the car on high-pollen days. A few more habits help a lot:

  • Check the daily pollen count and plan outdoor time for when it is lower — often after rain, and later in the day.
  • Shower and change clothes after being outside, so you do not carry pollen into your bed.
  • Dry laundry inside; clothes hung outdoors collect pollen.
  • Wear sunglasses and a hat outdoors to keep pollen out of your eyes and hair.

2. Start medicine early — before the peak. This is the step most people get wrong. Many wait until they feel miserable, but allergy medicines work best when you start them before the season peaks. ACAAI calls steroid nasal sprays (intranasal corticosteroids) the single most effective type of medicine for allergic rhinitis, and notes that antihistamines work best when taken before symptoms develop. Practical options include:

  • Non-drowsy antihistamines (like cetirizine, loratadine, or fexofenadine) for sneezing, itching, and a runny nose. Older allergy pills can make you sleepy — ask about the non-drowsy kind.
  • Steroid nasal sprays for a stuffy, blocked nose and inflammation. These take a few days to reach full effect, so start early.
  • Saline (salt water) rinses to gently flush pollen and mucus from your nose. Always use distilled, sterile, or previously boiled and cooled water.

Many of these are available without a prescription. If you are not sure what to pick, are pregnant, or take other medicines, ask a doctor or pharmacist first. Our team is happy to help you build a simple plan through our primary & allergy care services.

3. Find your triggers with allergy testing. If you do not know exactly what you are allergic to, testing can tell you. ACAAI describes skin testing as the easiest and most sensitive way to identify your triggers. Knowing whether it is tree pollen, grass, ragweed, dust mites, or something else helps you time your medicine and target your efforts. For people with heavy, year-round symptoms, allergy shots (immunotherapy) can slowly train the body to react less over time.

The asthma link: why this matters more for some

If you have asthma, controlling your allergies is even more important. Allergic asthma — asthma set off by things like pollen — is the most common type of asthma, according to AAFA. When you breathe in pollen, your immune system can cause the airways in your lungs to swell, which brings on coughing, wheezing, chest tightness, and trouble breathing.

Because the two conditions are so closely linked, AAFA advises people to treat both their allergies and their asthma together. A longer, stronger pollen season can mean more asthma flare-ups, so a good allergy plan is part of keeping your breathing safe. If you or your child has asthma and allergy season makes it worse, tell your doctor — your asthma plan may need an update before pollen peaks.

Key takeaways

  • Allergy seasons really are getting worse — starting earlier and lasting longer as the climate warms.
  • Hay fever means sneezing, a runny or stuffy nose, itchy watery eyes, and tiredness from poor sleep. It does not cause a fever and is not contagious.
  • Start allergy medicine early, before the season peaks — do not wait until you feel awful.
  • Steroid nasal sprays and non-drowsy antihistamines are safe, effective first choices; saline rinses help too.
  • Lower your exposure: close windows on high-pollen days, check pollen counts, and shower after being outside.
  • If you have asthma, controlling allergies is extra important to prevent flare-ups.

When to see a doctor

Mild allergies can often be handled at home. But it is time to see a doctor if your symptoms last for weeks and disrupt your sleep, work, or school; if over-the-counter medicines are not helping enough; if you are not sure whether it is allergies, a cold, or a sinus infection; or if you have asthma and it feels worse during pollen season. Get help right away for any trouble breathing, wheezing, or tightness in the chest.

Questions to ask your doctor

  • Do my symptoms sound like seasonal allergies, or could something else be causing them?
  • Which medicine is right for me, and when should I start taking it each year?
  • Are non-drowsy options and steroid nasal sprays safe with the other medicines I take?
  • Would allergy testing help me figure out my triggers?
  • I have asthma — how should I adjust my plan during pollen season?
  • Are allergy shots (immunotherapy) a good option for me?
