PCOS Symptoms That Often Go Undiagnosed

PCOS symptoms are missed for years. A doctor in Elmhurst, Queens explains the signs, the diagnostic criteria, testing, and treatment options.

By Dr. Ferdous Salauddin, MD, Internal Medicine, Elmhurst Medical · Medically reviewed by Keerthana Rajagopal, PA · Updated August 2026

Most people with polycystic ovary syndrome spend years being told their symptoms are separate, unrelated, and mostly their own fault. In one international survey of women with PCOS, 33.6% waited more than two years for a diagnosis and 47.1% saw three or more health professionals before anyone named it (Journal of Clinical Endocrinology & Metabolism). PCOS symptoms are common and testable — here is what actually gets missed.

What is PCOS, really?

PCOS is a hormonal and metabolic condition, not just an ovary problem. It affects 10–13% of reproductive-age women worldwide under current diagnostic criteria (2023 International Evidence-Based PCOS Guideline).

The name misleads in two ways. You do not need cysts — the "polycystic" appearance on ultrasound is one possible finding, not a requirement. And it is not confined to the reproductive system: it involves how your body handles insulin, which is why it touches weight, skin, hair, mood and long-term diabetes risk.

How is PCOS diagnosed? (The Rotterdam criteria in plain language)

For adults, diagnosis requires any two of these three, after other causes have been ruled out (2023 International PCOS Guideline):

  1. Signs of high androgens — either visible (coarse dark hair on the face, chest or abdomen; sometimes scalp thinning) or on a blood test.
  2. Irregular or absent ovulation — most often showing up as irregular cycles.
  3. Polycystic ovaries on ultrasound, or a raised AMH level (anti-Müllerian hormone, a blood test).

Two practical points that save time and money. First, if you already have both irregular cycles and signs of high androgens, an ultrasound is not necessary. Second, for adolescents both irregular cycles and high androgens are required, and ultrasound or AMH should not be used within eight years of a first period, because polycystic-looking ovaries are common and normal at that age.

"Irregular" has a definition. More than three years after your first period and before perimenopause, that means cycles shorter than 21 days or longer than 35 days, or fewer than eight cycles a year. Any single cycle longer than 90 days counts on its own, at any age. So does never having had a period by age 15 (2023 International PCOS Guideline).

Which PCOS symptoms do people miss?

  • Irregular or absent periods. Frequently normalized — by patients and clinicians. Many people spend a decade on birth control that masks the pattern entirely.
  • Adult acne that will not resolve, especially along the jawline and chin, and especially if it started or worsened in your twenties.
  • Hirsutism — coarse, dark hair on the upper lip, chin, chest, abdomen or thighs. Hirsutism prevalence is similar across ethnicities, though the scoring threshold used to define it varies from 4 to 6 depending on background.
  • Scalp hair thinning. Common, but on its own a weak predictor of high androgens — a clue that needs the rest of the picture. Our guide on why you might be losing your hair covers the other causes worth testing.
  • Weight changes and difficulty losing weight — though plenty of people with PCOS are at a normal weight.
  • Insulin resistance, which can show up as darkened velvety skin at the neck, armpits or groin (acanthosis nigricans), or as blood sugar creeping up on routine labs.
  • Anxiety and depression. Highly prevalent in PCOS; the guideline recommends screening everyone with PCOS for both.
  • Trouble conceiving. Often the first time anyone investigates seriously — far too late in the story.
  • Loud snoring and daytime exhaustion. Obstructive sleep apnea is more common in PCOS independent of body weight.

All of the above per the 2023 International PCOS Guideline.

Why is PCOS missed so often?

Because the symptoms present to different specialties. Acne goes to dermatology, irregular periods to gynecology, weight and blood sugar to primary care, mood to mental health. Nobody assembles the list.

The data is blunt. Beyond the delays above, only 35.2% of women were satisfied with how their diagnosis was handled and 15.6% with the information they received (Journal of Clinical Endocrinology & Metabolism). In an Australian community cohort, half of the women who met diagnostic criteria were still undiagnosed at age 35 (Human Reproduction, NIH/PMC). The 2023 guideline acknowledges that dissatisfaction with care is high and explicitly calls for reducing weight stigma (2023 International PCOS Guideline).

If you have been told to "just lose weight" and come back — that was not a plan.

Is PCOS different for South Asian women?

The evidence says yes, in ways that matter for how urgently you should get checked.

A UK study comparing South Asian and Caucasian women with PCOS found South Asian women presented significantly younger (26 ± 4 vs 30.1 ± 5 years), had irregular periods starting younger, and had markedly higher fasting insulin (89.4 vs 48.6 pmol/L) with lower insulin sensitivity and more severe symptoms (Clinical Endocrinology). A later review describes the same pattern — younger presentation, more hirsutism, acne, acanthosis nigricans and infertility — against a background population that already has higher rates of insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes (Health and Quality of Life Outcomes, NIH/PMC).

The global guideline also notes PCOS prevalence may be higher in South East Asian and Eastern Mediterranean regions than the 10–13% global figure (2023 International PCOS Guideline). The American Heart Association's statement on South Asians documents roughly two-fold higher type 2 diabetes prevalence and higher metabolic syndrome rates at every body mass index, with lower waist thresholds — above 90 cm in men and 80 cm in women (Circulation).

If you are from Bangladesh, India, Pakistan, Nepal or Sri Lanka: metabolic screening should not wait until you look "at risk" by the usual yardsticks.

What are the long-term health risks?

This is the honest reason a diagnosis is worth having, even if your symptoms feel manageable.

PCOS carries an increased risk of impaired fasting glucose, impaired glucose tolerance and type 2 diabetes regardless of age and body weight — which is why the guideline recommends repeat glucose testing every one to three years rather than a single check. Cardiovascular risk is also increased, although the guideline notes absolute risk in premenopausal women remains low. Endometrial hyperplasia and cancer risk are higher, though routine screening for them is not recommended (2023 International PCOS Guideline).

Knowing you have PCOS moves you from unexplained symptoms to a monitored, modifiable risk profile. That is a real gain.

What are the treatment options?

Lifestyle is recommended for everyone with PCOS — and benefits occur even without weight loss. No single diet has been shown to be superior. The activity target is 150–300 minutes a week of moderate activity, or 75–150 of vigorous, plus muscle-strengthening on two days.

Combined oral contraceptive pills are first-line for irregular cycles and unwanted hair growth, with the guideline favoring lower-dose estrogen (20–30 μg ethinylestradiol) and noting no advantage above 30 μg.

Metformin is recommended alongside lifestyle for weight, hormonal and metabolic outcomes, particularly at a BMI of 25 or above, started low and increased gradually to limit stomach upset.

For fertility, letrozole is first-line for ovulation induction. Most people with PCOS who want to conceive can — it may take help, and it usually takes a plan.

Cosmetic hair reduction, targeted acne treatment and mental health support are legitimate parts of care, not vanity. The guideline specifically recognizes that body image in PCOS is affected by weight, excess hair, acne and scalp thinning, and that this deserves attention rather than dismissal (2023 International PCOS Guideline).

When to see a doctor about this

Book a visit if you recognize any of these:

  • Cycles shorter than 21 days or longer than 35 days, or fewer than eight periods a year
  • Any single cycle longer than 90 days, or no period at all by age 15
  • New or worsening coarse, dark hair growth on the face, chest or abdomen
  • Adult acne not responding to over-the-counter treatment
  • Darkened, velvety skin patches on the neck, armpits or groin
  • Six months or more of trying to conceive without success, or irregular cycles plus pregnancy plans
  • A family history of PCOS or type 2 diabetes plus any of the above

Go to an emergency room for sudden severe pelvic pain with fever, fainting or vomiting, or for very heavy bleeding soaking through a pad an hour.

Frequently asked questions

Can I have PCOS with normal ovaries on ultrasound?+

Yes. Diagnosis needs two of three criteria, so irregular cycles plus high androgens is enough — and in that case an ultrasound is not even required (2023 International PCOS Guideline).

Can I have PCOS if I am thin?+

Yes. The guideline's glucose and lipid testing recommendations apply regardless of age and BMI, precisely because metabolic risk in PCOS is not limited to higher body weights (2023 International PCOS Guideline).

Does PCOS mean I cannot get pregnant?+

No. PCOS is a common cause of difficulty ovulating, and letrozole is recommended first-line for ovulation induction. Many people conceive with straightforward treatment; others need more support.

I was on the pill for years. Will that make diagnosis harder?+

It can, because the pill regulates bleeding and can lower androgen levels, hiding two of the three criteria. Tell your clinician how long you have been on it so testing can be timed sensibly.

How often should I be re-tested after diagnosis?+

Glucose status every one to three years, blood pressure annually, lipids from diagnosis, and mental health screening as part of routine care (2023 International PCOS Guideline).
