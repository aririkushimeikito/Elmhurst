How to Lower Your A1C: What Actually Works

How to lower your A1C, explained by a doctor in Elmhurst, Queens: what the number means, realistic timelines, and what actually moves it — including rice.

By Dr. Ferdous Salauddin, MD, Internal Medicine, Elmhurst Medical · Medically reviewed by Dr. Choudhury Hasan, MD · Updated August 2026

Someone handed you a lab result with a number on it — 5.9, or 6.4, or 7.8 — and told you it was "a little high." Now you are searching how to lower your A1C and getting a wall of contradictory advice, most of it telling you to stop eating the food you grew up on. Here is the honest version: the number is very movable, the timeline is about three months, and you do not have to give up rice.

What does A1C actually measure?

Glucose in your blood sticks to the hemoglobin in your red blood cells. The A1C test measures what percentage of your hemoglobin has sugar attached to it — an average of your blood sugar over the past two to three months, because red blood cells regenerate roughly every three months (CDC).

A fasting glucose is a snapshot; A1C is the trend. It needs no fasting, so you can get it on a walk-in visit at 6pm, and because it is weighted toward your recent glucose, change can show up in under three months (ADA Standards of Care 2026).

What do the A1C numbers mean?

Ranges and glucose conversions from the CDC and the ADA Standards of Care 2026.

Two things people are rarely told. A diagnosis normally requires two abnormal results, so one high number is not a life sentence (ADA). And once you have diabetes, the general goal is 7% or lower, though under 8% is reasonable for some people depending on age and other conditions (CDC).

Why South Asian and Latino patients in Queens should get tested earlier

This matters in our neighborhood. A national analysis found age- and sex-adjusted diabetes prevalence of 23.3% in South Asian adults, 24.6% in Mexican, 21.7% in Puerto Rican, and 20.5% in Cuban and Dominican adults — versus 12.1% in non-Hispanic white adults (JAMA, 2019). South Asians also develop type 2 diabetes earlier and at lower body weight, driven by lower insulin secretion, more insulin resistance, and more liver fat (Diabetes Care, 2024). And 37.9% of Asian adults with diabetes in that analysis did not know they had it (JAMA, 2019).

So the screening threshold is different for you. The ADA recommends testing adults with a BMI of 23 or higher if you are of Asian ancestry, versus 25 for everyone else, when one other risk factor is present (ADA). The USPSTF agrees, and also advises earlier screening in American Indian/Alaska Native, Black, Hispanic/Latino, and Pacific Islander adults (USPSTF).

How long does it take to lower your A1C?

Plan on three months to see a real change, and recheck at three-month intervals while you are actively working on it. Once you are at goal, twice a year is usually enough (CDC). Anyone promising a two-week A1C drop is selling something.

What actually lowers A1C, ranked by evidence

1. Structured nutrition change (biggest single lever)

Medical nutrition therapy with a dietitian produces absolute A1C reductions of up to 2.0 percentage points in type 2 diabetes at three to six months — comparable to or greater than medication. The ADA also states there is no single ideal eating pattern and no ideal macronutrient split: Mediterranean, vegetarian, low-fat, low-carb, and DASH are all acceptable. The common threads are more nonstarchy vegetables, fewer added sugars and refined grains, and less ultra-processed food (ADA Nutrition Therapy Consensus Report).

2. Weight loss, if you carry extra weight

For prediabetes, the target is 7–10% of body weight; for type 2 diabetes, 5% brings clinical benefit and 15% brings the best outcomes. In the DiRECT trial, remission rates climbed from 7% in people losing under 5% to 86% in those losing 15% or more (ADA).

3. Movement

Structured exercise training lowers A1C by about 0.67 percentage points on average (JAMA meta-analysis). A network meta-analysis ranked interval training highest at −0.78, then combined aerobic plus resistance at −0.54, yoga at −0.45, and steady aerobic at −0.42 (Diabetes Care). Timing is a free upgrade: walking soon after a meal blunts the spike more than walking before eating or not at all (Sports Medicine meta-analysis). Ten minutes around the block after dinner counts.

4. Sleep

In a meta-analysis of 20 studies, short sleep was associated with an A1C 0.23 percentage points higher and long sleep 0.13 higher than normal sleep, with poor sleep quality also linked to higher A1C (Diabetes Care). If you work nights, this is not a minor detail.

5. Medication, when it is the right call

Metformin has historically been first-line for type 2 diabetes: effective, inexpensive, weight-neutral, and it does not cause low blood sugar. Adding a GLP-1 receptor agonist or dual GIP/GLP-1 agonist usually lowers A1C by 1 to more than 2 percentage points, and the ADA is clear the choice should be a shared decision weighing heart, kidney, and weight goals, cost, and tolerance (ADA Standards of Care 2026, Section 9). In prevention trials metformin cut progression to diabetes by 31% — real, but less than the 58% from lifestyle change (CDC National DPP).

Rice, roti, and tortillas: the practical version

Nobody is going to tell you to stop eating rice. But quantity matters. In a meta-analysis of over 577,000 people, higher white rice intake was associated with a 16% higher risk of type 2 diabetes, and above roughly 300 g of cooked rice a day each additional serving added about 13% more risk; brown rice went the other way, at 11% lower risk (BMJ Open, 2022). White rice has a glycemic index around 73 versus about 68 for brown (Harvard Health).

What to actually do:

  • Shrink the rice, not the meal. Keep the curry, dal, sabzi, beans, and meat; halve the rice and fill that space with vegetables.
  • Add dal, chana, rajma, or black beans. A randomized trial found a low-glycemic legume diet lowered A1C by 0.5 percentage points in three months, more than a high-wheat-fiber diet (JAMA Internal Medicine).
  • Make at least half your grains whole and intact, and aim for at least 14 g of fiber per 1,000 calories; intake above 50 g/day is linked to A1C reductions of 0.2–0.3 points (ADA). Whole wheat roti over paratha; corn tortillas over flour.
  • Drop the sugary drinks first. In adults with prediabetes, one or more sugar-sweetened drinks a day was associated with 26% higher diabetes risk, and swapping them for water lowered risk by 7–8% (ADA). Mango lassi, sweet chai, horchata, bubble tea, soda — highest-yield swap on the list.

Common myths about lowering A1C

"Prediabetes means I will definitely get diabetes." No. The Diabetes Prevention Program — 7% weight loss plus 150 minutes of activity a week — cut progression by 58% over three years, 71% in people over 60, and incidence was still 27% lower fifteen years later (CDC).

"I need to eat keto." Very-low-carb eating does lower A1C, strongest at 3 and 6 months — but the benefit was not sustained at 12 to 24 months, largely because people stop. Two systematic reviews also found no significant A1C effect from glycemic index alone (ADA).

"The number is always accurate." Not always. Anemia, iron deficiency, recent blood loss or transfusion, sickle cell trait, thalassemia, kidney failure, liver disease, pregnancy, and some medications can falsely raise or lower A1C (CDC) — and hemoglobin variants are not rare in South Asian and Mediterranean families. If your A1C does not match your glucose readings, say so; fasting glucose or a glucose tolerance test can be used instead (ADA).

When to see a doctor about this

Get seen promptly if you have:

  • An A1C of 6.5% or higher on any test — it needs confirming and a plan
  • An A1C of 5.7–6.4% with no follow-up plan; this is where prevention works best
  • Increased thirst, frequent urination, unexplained weight loss, blurry vision, or persistent fatigue
  • Numbness, tingling, or burning in your feet, or a foot sore that is not healing
  • Never been tested and are 35 or older, or are South Asian, Latino, Black, Native American, or Pacific Islander with a BMI of 23 or higher plus another risk factor (USPSTF)

Call 911 for confusion, vomiting that will not stop, deep rapid breathing, fruity-smelling breath, or a glucose reading you cannot bring down — these can signal a diabetic emergency.

Frequently asked questions

Can I lower my A1C without medication?+

Often, yes — especially with prediabetes, where structured lifestyle change reduced progression by 58% (CDC). With established diabetes it depends on your A1C, your other conditions, and how fast the number needs to come down.

Do I have to give up rice to lower my A1C?+

No. Reduce the portion, favor brown or other whole intact grains where you can, and fill the space with dal, beans, and vegetables (BMJ Open, JAMA Internal Medicine).

Does fasting before the test lower my A1C?+

No. A1C reflects two to three months of glucose (CDC).

How often should I get an A1C test?+

Normal: at least every three years. Prediabetes: yearly. Diabetes: at least twice a year, and every three months if you are not at goal or just changed treatment (ADA).
