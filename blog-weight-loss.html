A Real, Medical Approach to Weight Loss & Obesity

If you have tried to lose weight and it keeps coming back, it is not because you failed. Obesity is a medical condition — and, like high blood pressure or diabetes, it responds to the right plan and support.

Obesity is a medical condition, not a lack of willpower

Let us start with the most important thing. Carrying extra weight is not a sign of being lazy or weak. The World Health Organization calls obesity a "chronic, relapsing disease" — meaning it is a long-term health condition that can come and go, and that needs real care over time, not just more effort (WHO). Doctors define obesity as a body mass index (BMI) of 30 or higher. BMI is a quick number that compares your weight to your height. It is not perfect, but it helps us start the conversation.

You are far from alone. About 4 in 10 American adults live with obesity, based on the most recent national health survey (CDC, 2021–2023). Around the world, roughly 1 in 8 people now live with obesity (WHO). This is a shared, human problem — and there is a lot we can do about it.

40.3% of U.S. adults have obesity, and this rate has held steady over the past decade — a sign that willpower alone was never the answer (CDC).

Why weight is so hard to control

Your body works hard to hold on to weight. Many things you cannot simply "choose" your way out of are at play:

  • Genes and biology. Weight runs in families. WHO describes obesity as coming from complex links between genetics, brain chemistry, and eating behavior (WHO).
  • Hormones and hunger signals. Hormones tell your brain when you are hungry or full. When these signals are off, you may feel hungry even after eating.
  • Sleep and stress. Short sleep and constant stress can raise hunger and make the body store more fat.
  • Medical conditions. An underactive thyroid (thyroid disease) and insulin resistance from conditions like PCOS/PMOS can slow weight loss and drive weight gain.
  • Medications. Some medicines — for mood, blood sugar, blood pressure, or allergies — can add weight as a side effect.

This is why two people can eat the same food and move the same amount, yet have very different bodies. It is biology, not blame.

Small, steady food changes that actually last

Crash diets promise fast results, but they rarely stick — and the weight usually returns. What works long-term is a set of small changes you can keep for years. You do not have to give up rice, roti, or dal. It is about how the plate is built, not banning your family's food.

  • Try the plate method. Picture your plate in parts: fill half with vegetables (bhindi, saag, salad, sabzi), one quarter with protein (dal, beans, fish, chicken, paneer, eggs), and one quarter with rice or roti. Same foods — better balance.
  • Add protein and fiber. These keep you full longer, so you naturally eat less without feeling hungry. Beans, lentils, eggs, yogurt, and vegetables are simple, low-cost choices.
  • Watch the portion of rice and bread. You do not have to skip them. Serving a little less — one roti instead of two, or a smaller scoop of rice — adds up gently over time.
  • Cut back on sugary drinks. Soda, sweet tea, mango lassi, and juice add a lot of sugar fast. The American Heart Association suggests keeping added sugar under about 6 teaspoons a day for women and 9 for men — and one can of soda alone has about 10 (AHA). Water, unsweetened chai, or seltzer are easy swaps.

Notice these are gentle shifts, not punishments. You should still enjoy your food and your culture.

Movement you will actually keep doing

The best exercise is the one you will do again tomorrow. You do not need a gym. A short walk after meals is one of the simplest habits — it can help steady your blood sugar and clear your head. Over a week, national guidelines suggest aiming for about 150 minutes of moderate activity, which is roughly a 20–25 minute walk most days (NIDDK). Walk with a family member, get off the train one stop early, or pace while on the phone. Start small and build up.

Sleep and stress matter more than you think

When life is busy — long shifts, small children, money worries — sleep and calm are usually the first things to go. But poor sleep and high stress raise hunger hormones and make weight harder to lose. Protecting 7–8 hours of sleep, and finding small ways to lower stress (prayer, a walk, time with people you love), is real weight-loss work, not a luxury.

Treating the hidden barriers

Sometimes weight will not budge because of a medical roadblock. This is where seeing a doctor truly helps. A simple set of blood tests can check for problems that quietly work against you — such as a slow thyroid, insulin resistance, or prediabetes. Treating these can make everything else work better. It is also worth reviewing your medicine list, because a small change chosen with your doctor can sometimes ease weight gain. You are not doing something wrong; there may simply be a barrier we can remove together.

When medical options can help

Food and movement are the foundation, but they are not the whole toolbox. When lifestyle changes are not enough, medical care can add real support.

Nutrition counseling. For adults with obesity, the U.S. Preventive Services Task Force gives a strong recommendation (a "B" grade) to intensive, multi-part behavioral programs — regular sessions over a year or more that combine eating changes, activity, and coaching to solve real-life barriers (USPSTF). Our team offers this kind of obesity & nutrition counseling in a warm, judgment-free way.

GLP-1 medications. You may have heard of newer medicines like semaglutide (Wegovy) or tirzepatide (Zepbound). These are called GLP-1 medications. They copy a natural gut hormone that tells your brain you are full, so you feel less hungry (NIDDK). They are usually offered to adults with a BMI of 30 or higher, or 27 and above with a weight-related condition. On average, people lose an added 3% to 12% of their starting weight compared with lifestyle changes alone (NIDDK). They are not magic, and they are not for everyone — they work best alongside healthy eating and activity, and they must be prescribed and followed by a doctor. If you are curious, that is a great conversation to have with us.

Why crash diets backfire — and why small wins are enough

Very strict, very fast diets usually fail because the body fights back with more hunger and a slower metabolism. And here is the hopeful part: you do not need to reach a "perfect" weight to feel better. Losing just 5% to 7% of your starting weight can help prevent or delay type 2 diabetes, and even a 3% to 5% loss can lower fat in the liver and improve blood pressure (NIDDK). A modest first goal — about 5% over six months — is exactly what specialists recommend (NIDDK). For someone who weighs 200 pounds, that is just 10 pounds — and it already protects your health.

Key takeaways

  • Obesity is a medical condition shaped by genes, hormones, sleep, stress, and other health issues — not a character flaw.
  • Small, lasting changes beat crash diets: the plate method, more protein and fiber, fewer sugary drinks, and a walk after meals.
  • Hidden barriers like thyroid disease, insulin resistance, or certain medications can be checked and treated.
  • Nutrition counseling and, when right for you, GLP-1 medications under a doctor's care can add real help.
  • You do not need to be perfect. Losing even 5% of your weight meaningfully improves your health.

Questions to ask your doctor

  • Could a thyroid problem, insulin resistance, or prediabetes be making weight loss harder for me?
  • Do any of my current medications tend to cause weight gain, and is there another option?
  • What is a realistic, healthy weight goal for me over the next six months?
  • Am I a good candidate for nutrition counseling or a GLP-1 medication?
  • What blood tests do you recommend to understand my full picture?
  • How will we track progress together, beyond just the number on the scale?
