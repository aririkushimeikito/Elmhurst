Men's health

The Health Checks Every Man Over 30 Should Get

Men's health screening in your 30s, 40s, and 50s: blood pressure, lipids, A1C, colon and prostate screening, mental health. From doctors in Elmhurst, Queens.

By Dr. Choudhury Hasan, MD, Internal Medicine, Elmhurst Medical · Medically reviewed by Dr. Ferdous Salauddin, MD · Updated August 2026

Most men we see for the first time in their forties are not sick. They're overdue — blood pressure that's been creeping up for a decade, an A1C nobody has checked, a symptom they've quietly ignored for years.

Men's health screening in your 30s isn't about doing everything. It's about doing the few things that change outcomes, at the right ages. Here's the schedule, built on current US Preventive Services Task Force and specialty guideline recommendations.

Why do men put this off so long?

It isn't laziness. In a Cleveland Clinic survey of about 1,174 US men, 72% said they would rather do household chores than go to the doctor, only half considered an annual check-up part of taking care of themselves, and one in five admitted they haven't been fully honest with their doctor. In 2012, US women made preventive care visits at 76.6 per 100 people versus 45.4 for men.

And if you work six days a week and the clinic closes at 5pm, that's not avoidance, that's arithmetic. Which is why we're open until 7:30pm, Monday through Saturday.

Blood pressure: the cheapest thing that saves your life

The USPSTF gives blood pressure screening its strongest rating (Grade A), recommending annual screening at 40 and older or at increased risk and every 3 to 5 years for lower-risk adults 18–39. Increased risk includes being Black, overweight or obese, or having high-normal readings. One high reading isn't a diagnosis — confirm with home measurements before treating.

The American Heart Association grades readings as normal (under 120/80), elevated (120–129 systolic), stage 1 (130–139 or 80–89), and stage 2 (140+ or 90+).

Cholesterol and heart risk

The statin conversation formally starts at 40. The USPSTF recommends a statin for adults 40 to 75 with at least one risk factor and 10-year cardiovascular risk of 10% or more (Grade B), and selective offering at 7.5% to under 10% (Grade C).

But get lipids earlier if family history warrants it — especially South Asian men, who have at least twice the prevalence of type 2 diabetes as non-Hispanic whites and, per an American Heart Association scientific statement, tend to have heart attacks at younger ages, which standard risk calculators underestimate.

Diabetes: screening starts at 35 — earlier for many of our patients

The USPSTF recommends screening adults 35 to 70 with overweight or obesity (Grade B). Critically, it says to consider earlier screening in people of Asian American, Hispanic/Latino, Black, American Indian/Alaska Native, or Pacific Islander background, or with a family history — and to use a lower BMI cutoff of 23 for Asian American patients.

Colorectal cancer screening: 45, not 50

Many men missed this memo. Screening now starts at age 45 (Grade B for 45–49, A for 50–75). Colonoscopy is one option; stool-based tests on schedule are another. The best test is the one you'll actually complete.

Prostate and PSA: a genuine judgment call

Here's the honest version. For men 55 to 69, the USPSTF says the PSA decision should be an individual one (Grade C), and clinicians should not screen men who don't express a preference. After 70, it recommends against PSA screening (Grade D).

The trade-offs are real: false positives, biopsies, and overdiagnosis leading to treatment of cancers that would never have caused harm, with risks of incontinence and erectile dysfunction. The benefit is a small reduction in prostate cancer death.

Risk isn't uniform, though. The Task Force notes the lifetime risk of dying of prostate cancer is 4.2% for African American men versus 2.3% for white men, with mortality of 44.1 versus 19.1 per 100,000. It couldn't issue a separate recommendation for African American men, but says informing them of that increased risk is reasonable. If you're Black or have a father or brother with prostate cancer, start this conversation before 55.

Testicles: awareness, not a ritual exam

The USPSTF actually recommends against routine testicular cancer screening in men without symptoms, by clinician exam or self-exam (Grade D). But testicular cancer is the most common cancer in males 15 to 34 — so know what normal feels like, and get any new lump, heaviness, or size change looked at promptly.

STIs: not just for 20-somethings

The CDC recommends HIV screening at least once for everyone 13 to 64 and hepatitis C testing once for all adults over 18. For lower-risk heterosexual men, evidence doesn't support routine chlamydia and gonorrhea screening, though it's worth considering in higher-prevalence settings. Men who have sex with men should be screened for chlamydia, gonorrhea, syphilis, and HIV at least annually, and every 3 to 6 months at higher risk.

Mental health

The USPSTF recommends screening all adults for depression (Grade B) and adults up to 64 for anxiety (Grade B). In men it often shows up as irritability, drinking more, sleeping badly, or losing interest — not as sadness. If you'd rather read first, start with talking to your primary care doctor about mental health.

Should I get my testosterone checked?

Be careful here — this is the most oversold test in men's health.

The American Urological Association calls total testosterone below 300 ng/dL a reasonable cutoff, but diagnosis requires two separate early-morning measurements on the same lab assay, plus actual symptoms. A single afternoon draw diagnoses nothing.

The AUA also documents how sloppy real-world practice has become: testing and prescriptions have nearly tripled in recent years, up to 25% of men on therapy never had a level checked first, nearly half never have it rechecked, and up to a third don't meet diagnostic criteria. The AUA ties part of the surge to direct-to-consumer advertising and anti-aging clinics.

Two more things. The AUA says we cannot definitively state whether testosterone therapy increases or decreases cardiovascular events, and the FDA requires labeling about a possible increased risk of heart attack and stroke. Testosterone also suppresses sperm production — the AUA says not to prescribe it to men trying to conceive.

Fatigue, low libido, and low mood are worth investigating — they're just usually not testosterone. Sleep apnea, depression, thyroid disease, anemia, and uncontrolled diabetes are more common, and our guide on blood tests to ask for when you're always tired covers that workup.

Why we ask about erectile dysfunction

Because it's a cardiovascular warning sign, not just a bedroom problem. In the MESA study of 1,757 men with no prior cardiovascular event, hard cardiovascular events occurred in 6.3% of men with ED versus 2.6% without, and ED stayed an independent predictor after full adjustment (hazard ratio 1.9). The UK's QRISK calculator added ED as a risk factor in 2017; US guidelines haven't.

So when we ask, we're screening your arteries. It's a medical question, and there's nothing embarrassing about the answer.

When to see a doctor about this

Don't wait for a birthday if you have:

  • Chest pain or pressure, especially with exertion — call 911
  • A reading above 180/120 with chest pain, shortness of breath, back pain, weakness, numbness, vision change, or trouble speaking — the AHA says call 911
  • Blood in your stool or urine, or bowel changes lasting weeks
  • A new testicular lump, heaviness, or size change
  • Unexplained weight loss, night sweats, or worsening fatigue
  • Thoughts of harming yourself — call or text 988 right now

Frequently asked questions

Is a PSA test a bad idea?+

No — it's a decision, not a default. Between 55 and 69 the USPSTF frames it as an individual choice after discussing benefits and harms, and recommends against it after 70.

Do I need a colonoscopy at 45, or is a stool test enough?+

Both are acceptable under the USPSTF recommendation. Stool tests must be repeated on schedule, and an abnormal result means a colonoscopy follows.

I saw an ad for testosterone therapy. Is that legitimate?+

Some clinics are, but the AUA requires two early-morning tests plus symptoms before diagnosis — and notes up to a third of men on therapy never met criteria.

I have no insurance. Can I still get screened?+

Yes. We accept cash alongside all major insurance plans, and no coverage shouldn't keep you from a blood pressure check and basic labs.
