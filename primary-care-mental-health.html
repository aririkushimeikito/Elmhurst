Mental health

You Can Talk to Your Primary Care Doctor About Mental Health

Primary care mental health support in Elmhurst, Queens: what your PCP can treat, what PHQ-9 and GAD-7 mean, privacy, medication myths, and crisis resources.

By Keerthana Rajagopal, PA, Elmhurst Medical · Medically reviewed by Dr. Choudhury Hasan, MD · Updated August 2026

Plenty of people come in for headaches, stomach pain, or "just feeling tired" and only mention what's really going on as they stand up to leave. You don't have to wait for the doorknob. Primary care mental health support is real care — not a consolation prize for people who can't reach a psychiatrist.

What does primary care mental health support actually include?

More than most people expect. In the US, primary care providers prescribe 79% of antidepressant medications and see 60% of people treated for depression. That isn't a workaround; it's how the system is designed. Here's what a primary care office does:

  • Screening. The US Preventive Services Task Force recommends screening all adults for depression (Grade B) and adults up to 64 for anxiety (Grade B). Routine care, not a special request.
  • Ruling out medical causes. Thyroid disease, anemia, B12 deficiency, and sleep apnea can all mimic depression.
  • Diagnosis and first-line treatment. For mild to moderate depression and anxiety: counseling, structured follow-up, sometimes medication.
  • Prescribing and monitoring. We start medication, watch how you respond, and adjust.
  • Referral. When specialty care is right, we make the connection instead of leaving you to search.

There's good evidence this works. A Cochrane review of 79 randomized trials with 24,308 patients found collaborative care produced greater improvement in depression than usual care, with benefits holding out to two years, and improved anxiety outcomes too.

What does the conversation actually look like?

Step by step, so nothing is a surprise.

  1. You say something. As small as "I haven't felt like myself," or "my chest gets tight for no reason." No clinical language needed.
  2. We ask questions. How long, how often, what changed, how it's affecting work, sleep, and family.
  3. You may fill out a short questionnaire. Usually the PHQ-9, the GAD-7, or both.
  4. We check the physical side. Bloodwork if the picture suggests thyroid, anemia, or vitamin causes.
  5. We make a plan together. Therapy, medication, both, or watchful follow-up — your call.
  6. We follow up. Usually in 2 to 6 weeks, not "come back if it worsens."

What are the PHQ-9 and GAD-7?

Two short questionnaires. Not tests you can fail.

The PHQ-9 asks nine questions about the last two weeks — interest, mood, sleep, energy, appetite, feelings of failure, concentration, restlessness, and thoughts of self-harm. Each scores 0 to 3, totaling 0 to 27. In the validation study of 580 primary care patients, scores of 5, 10, 15, and 20 marked mild, moderate, moderately severe, and severe depression, and a score of 10 or more had 88% sensitivity and 88% specificity for major depression.

The GAD-7 does the same for anxiety with seven questions. In its validation study, it identified generalized anxiety disorder with 89% sensitivity and 82% specificity at the cut point.

Mostly the score is a baseline, so weeks from now we can tell whether things are improving.

When anxiety and depression show up as body symptoms

Common, and not "in your head." In a study of 917 primary care patients who came in with at least one physical symptom, 20.0% had depression, 15.5% had anxiety, and 15.1% had a somatoform disorder — and each additional psychosocial stressor raised the likelihood 2.2-fold.

What you feel in your body What it can also be
Chest tightness, racing heart Panic or anxiety (after cardiac causes are ruled out)
Stomach pain, nausea, IBS-type symptoms Anxiety, chronic stress
Headaches, jaw or neck tension Anxiety, poor sleep, teeth grinding
Dizziness, shortness of breath, tingling hands Hyperventilation with panic
Exhaustion no amount of sleep fixes Depression, or a medical cause — see blood tests to ask for when you're always tired

We rule out physical causes first — chest pain always gets evaluated. But if the workup is clean and the symptom keeps returning, the next question is a fair one to ask.

Is what I say confidential?

Yes. The HIPAA Privacy Rule protects your health information, and the Department of Health and Human Services confirms it covers mental health and substance use information like any other medical information. Substance use treatment records in federally assisted programs get even stronger protection under the rule known as Part 2.

Practically: we don't discuss your visit with your spouse, parents, employer, or anyone else without permission, with narrow exceptions for immediate safety. Your immigration status is not a factor in your care.

Talking about this in a South Asian, Latino, or immigrant family

In many of the families we care for, mental health isn't discussed outside the home. Sometimes not inside it either.

That silence has measurable consequences. Among US adults with any mental illness between 2015 and 2019, 22.5% of Asian adults and 32.5% of Hispanic or Latino adults received any mental health services, versus 48.8% of white adults. Among those with unmet need, stigma — what neighbors would think, or the effect on a job — was cited by 45.2% of Asian adults and 36.9% of Hispanic adults.

Language matters just as much. A review of 18 studies found that in 15 of them, limited language proficiency was significantly associated with fewer mental health visits.

Which is the practical reason we say this: describing sadness, shame, or grief in your second language is genuinely harder. Our providers speak Bengali, Hindi, Urdu, Spanish, and English — use whichever lets you say what you actually mean.

Medication: separating myth from fact

Common worry What the evidence says
"They'll change my personality." The goal is you, functioning — not sedation. Side effects like upset stomach or headache are usually mild and tend to fade, per NIMH.
"They're addictive." A review of the evidence concludes antidepressants have no addictive property, though stopping abruptly can cause withdrawal — which is why doses are lowered slowly.
"I'll be on them forever." You revisit duration together. NIMH advises never stopping on your own; the dose is decreased slowly.
"It didn't work in a week." Antidepressants usually take 4 to 8 weeks, with sleep, appetite, and energy often improving before mood.
"Anxiety pills are all the same." No. Benzodiazepines are short-term and can cause tolerance and dependence; buspirone must be taken daily for 3 to 4 weeks and doesn't work as-needed, per NIMH.

One safety point NIMH emphasizes: people under 25 may have an increase in suicidal thoughts or behavior in the first weeks of an antidepressant or after a dose change, so anyone starting one needs close early monitoring. That's why we book the follow-up before you leave.

When is a specialist the right call?

Primary care handles a lot, not everything. We refer for bipolar disorder or psychosis, symptoms that haven't responded to two or more medication trials, substance use alongside a mental health condition, eating disorders, ongoing suicidal thoughts, or when you want ongoing therapy — a specialist's job. A referral isn't a rejection; we stay involved and coordinate.

When to see a doctor about this

Make an appointment if, for two weeks or more, you've had low mood or loss of interest, worry you can't switch off, sleep or appetite changes, trouble functioning, or unexplained physical symptoms.

Get help immediately if you have:

  • Thoughts of suicide or self-harm — call or text 988
  • A plan or the means to hurt yourself — call 911 or go to the nearest ER
  • Thoughts of harming someone else
  • Hearing or seeing things others don't
  • Not eating or drinking, or being unable to care for yourself

Crisis resources

  • 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline — call or text 988, or chat at 988lifeline.org. Free, confidential, 24/7/365, with judgment-free counselors for mental health struggles, emotional distress, or alcohol and drug concerns.
  • NYC 988 — the same number connects New Yorkers to free crisis counseling, mental health and substance use support, and referrals matched to your location, language, and insurance. NYC Health notes phone support in more than 200 languages, text and chat in English and Spanish, and no insurance requirement.
  • 911 — for an immediate, life-threatening emergency.

Frequently asked questions

Can my primary care provider prescribe antidepressants?+

Yes. Primary care providers write 79% of antidepressant prescriptions in the US and manage follow-up and dose adjustments.

Will this affect my job or immigration case?+

Your visit is protected health information under HIPAA, including mental health details. We don't share it with employers or family without your permission.

Do I have to take medication if I bring this up?+

No. Many people start with therapy, sleep and stress changes, and follow-up. Medication is one option among several, and it's your decision.

I'm not sure it's "bad enough" to mention.+

That's the most common reason people wait. A two-minute questionnaire is a cheap way to find out, and there's no threshold of suffering you must clear first. We take cash as well as insurance, so cost shouldn't stop you either.
