The Screenings You Need in Your 30s, 40s, and 50s

A clear guide to health screenings by age — blood pressure, diabetes, colon, breast and cervical cancer — with tables for men and women, from Elmhurst, Queens.

By Dr. Choudhury Hasan, MD, Internal Medicine, Elmhurst Medical · Medically reviewed by Keerthana Rajagopal, PA · Updated August 2026

Most people who walk into my office feeling fine are still overdue for something. Not because they are careless — because nobody ever handed them the list. So here is the list: health screenings by age, decade by decade, with the specific ages and intervals from current national guidelines and a note about what changes if you have a family history or a risk factor.

Two things to know before the tables. First, one of these ages moved recently: colon cancer screening now starts at 45, not 50. Second, most of this should cost you nothing.

What health screenings by age do I actually need?

The backbone of this list is the U.S. Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF), which grades preventive services A or B when the benefit is clear. Where a specialty society — the American Cancer Society, American Diabetes Association, American Heart Association — recommends something different, I have said so rather than pretending the guidelines all agree. They do not always, and you deserve to know that.

Which colon cancer test should I pick — and why 45?

The USPSTF recommends screening every adult from 45 through 75, grading it B for ages 45–49 and A for 50–75 (USPSTF). The American Cancer Society also starts at 45 (ACS).

You have real choices, and the best test is the one you will actually do:

  • FIT (stool test at home) — every year
  • Stool DNA-FIT — every 1 to 3 years
  • Colonoscopy — every 10 years
  • CT colonography or flexible sigmoidoscopy — every 5 years
  • Flexible sigmoidoscopy every 10 years plus annual FIT

Any abnormal stool, CT, or sigmoidoscopy result has to be followed by a colonoscopy — that is how the benefit actually gets delivered (USPSTF). If you have a first-degree relative who had colorectal cancer or advanced polyps, Lynch syndrome, familial adenomatous polyposis, or inflammatory bowel disease, the average-risk schedule does not apply to you — you likely need to start earlier and go more often.

Where guidelines genuinely disagree

I would rather tell you this than let you find out in a comment section.

  • Mammograms in your 40s. USPSTF says start at 40, every 2 years (USPSTF, 2024). The American Cancer Society says women 40–44 should have the choice to start annual mammograms, and that women 45–54 should be screened every year (ACS). Both are defensible. Yearly finds slightly more, at the cost of more false alarms and biopsies. This is a conversation, not a decree.
  • Cervical cancer starting age. USPSTF starts at 21 with a Pap (USPSTF); ACS starts at 25 and prefers a primary HPV test every 5 years (ACS).
  • Osteoporosis in men. The USPSTF says the evidence is insufficient — which is explicitly not a recommendation against screening, just a call for more research (USPSTF).

What if I have a family history, or my ethnicity changes the math?

Very often it does, and this matters a great deal in Elmhurst.

If you are South Asian — Bangladeshi, Pakistani, Indian, Nepali, Sri Lankan — your diabetes risk starts at a lower body weight than the standard cutoffs assume. Both the USPSTF and the ADA recommend using a BMI of 23 rather than 25 as the trigger for diabetes screening in Asian American adults (USPSTF). Diabetes prevalence in South Asian adults in the US has been measured at 23.3%, versus 12.1% in non-Hispanic White adults (Diabetes Care, 2024). Heart disease also arrives earlier, and standard US risk calculators tend to underestimate it in South Asian patients (American Heart Association, Circulation).

Other things that shift your schedule:

  • The USPSTF advises considering earlier diabetes screening in Asian American, Black, Hispanic/Latino, American Indian/Alaska Native and Native Hawaiian/Pacific Islander patients, and in anyone with a family history of diabetes, prior gestational diabetes, or PCOS (USPSTF).
  • Annual blood pressure checks — not every 3 to 5 years — are advised for adults 18–39 who are Black, overweight or obese, or already have high-normal readings (USPSTF).
  • Black men, and men whose father or brother had prostate cancer before 65, should start the PSA conversation at 45 instead of 50 (ACS).
  • A strong family history of breast or ovarian cancer may mean earlier mammograms plus MRI. Bring the details — ages at diagnosis matter.

Which vaccines belong on this list?

Screening is only half of prevention. From the CDC adult immunization schedule (CDC):

  • Influenza: 1 dose every year, every adult.
  • COVID-19: per the current season's recommendation.
  • Tdap/Td: a Tdap once, then a Td or Tdap booster every 10 years — and 1 dose of Tdap during each pregnancy.
  • HPV: through age 26; ages 27–45 by shared decision with your clinician.
  • Shingles (recombinant zoster): 2 doses starting at 50 (earlier if immunocompromised).
  • Pneumococcal: recommended for adults 50 and older who have not had a pneumococcal conjugate vaccine, and earlier if you have certain risk conditions (CDC).
  • MMR and varicella: if you do not have documented immunity.
  • RSV: 75 and older, and 50–74 with risk factors.

Do I have to pay for any of this?

Usually not. Most health plans, including Marketplace plans, must cover a set of preventive services — immunizations and screening tests among them — at no cost to you, even if you have not met your deductible, when you use an in-network provider (HealthCare.gov). That coverage is tied to services the USPSTF grades A or B, which is most of what is in the tables above.

Two honest caveats. Coverage can vary, and $0 is not guaranteed in every situation (HealthCare.gov) — a screening colonoscopy that becomes a polyp removal, or a diagnostic mammogram ordered because you found a lump, can be billed differently than pure screening. Ask before, not after. And if you have no insurance at all, ask us about cash pricing — a blood pressure cuff and a finger-stick glucose do not need to wait for paperwork.

When to see a doctor about this

Do not wait for your next birthday if any of these apply:

  • You are 45 or older and have never had a colon cancer screening of any kind.
  • You are 40 or older and have never had a mammogram, or it has been more than 2 years.
  • You have never had a hepatitis C test or an HIV test — both are one-time tests for most adults.
  • Your blood pressure has read 130/80 or higher anywhere, including a pharmacy machine. Confirmation outside the office is now part of the recommended process (USPSTF).
  • You are 35 to 70 with a BMI over 25 — or over 23 if you are Asian American — and have never had a diabetes test.
  • You are 50 to 80, smoke or quit within the past 15 years, and have a 20 pack-year history. Yearly low-dose CT is recommended and it saves lives (USPSTF).
  • You have felt persistently low, hopeless, or anxious for more than two weeks. Depression and anxiety screening are Grade B recommendations for a reason — this is medical, not a character flaw.
  • Any new lump, unexplained weight loss, blood in stool or urine, or a cough that will not quit. Those are not screening questions. Those are "come in this week" questions.

Frequently asked questions

At what age does colon cancer screening start now?+

45 for adults at average risk — the USPSTF grades it B for ages 45–49 and A for 50–75, and the American Cancer Society also starts at 45 (USPSTF). If you have a family history, you may need to start earlier.

How often do I need a mammogram in my 40s?+

The USPSTF recommends every 2 years from age 40 to 74 (USPSTF, 2024). The American Cancer Society offers annual screening as a choice from 40 and recommends annual from 45 to 54 (ACS). Either is a reasonable plan.

How often should I get my cholesterol checked?+

There is no single fixed interval. Between 40 and 75, the point of checking is to estimate your 10-year cardiovascular risk; a statin is recommended if you have at least one risk factor and a 10-year risk of 10% or more (USPSTF). In practice most adults get a lipid panel every few years, sooner if something is off.

Do I need a Pap smear every year?+

No. From 21 to 29, a Pap every 3 years. From 30 to 65, a Pap every 3 years, an HPV test every 5 years, or both together every 5 years (USPSTF). Annual Paps are no longer recommended for average-risk women.

Are preventive screenings really free?+

Most plans must cover recommended preventive services with no copay or coinsurance even before your deductible is met, when you stay in network — though coverage varies and $0 is not guaranteed in every case (HealthCare.gov).

I feel completely fine. Do I still need any of this?+

Yes — that is the entire point. High blood pressure, prediabetes, high cholesterol, hepatitis C, and early colon polyps all feel like nothing at all. Screening is what you do instead of waiting for a symptom.
