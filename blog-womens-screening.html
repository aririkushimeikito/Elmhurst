Women's Health Screenings: What You Need and When

Screenings are quick, simple checks that find problems early — often before you feel anything. Here is a clear list of what to get and when, with trusted sources for every step.

Most women's health screenings take only a few minutes. They are tests done when you feel fine, to catch a problem early — when it is small and easier to treat. You do not need to get all of them at once. Your doctor helps you build a simple plan based on your age, your health, and your family history.

This guide walks through the main screenings, one by one. For each one, we list the age to start and how often to repeat it, with a link to the group that makes the rule. You can bookmark this page and bring your questions to your next visit.

Checks for your heart and blood sugar

Blood pressure. This is the quick arm-cuff test. High blood pressure (also called hypertension) usually has no signs, so a regular check is the only way to know. The U.S. Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF), a panel of independent experts, recommends a blood pressure check for all adults age 18 and older. If you are 40 or older, or at higher risk, you should be checked every year. Younger women with normal readings can be checked every 3 to 5 years (USPSTF, hypertension screening).

Cholesterol. Cholesterol (a fatty substance in your blood) can raise your risk of heart disease if it is too high. It is measured with a simple blood test. Many groups suggest women start checking around age 20 and repeat it every 4 to 6 years, or more often if you have risk factors like diabetes or a family history (MedlinePlus, health screenings for women).

Diabetes (A1c or blood sugar). Diabetes means your blood sugar is too high. It is checked with a blood test, often the A1c (a number that shows your average blood sugar over about 3 months). The USPSTF recommends screening for prediabetes and type 2 diabetes in adults age 35 to 70 who are overweight, usually every 3 years if results are normal (USPSTF, diabetes screening). This matters for our community. The same guidance notes that South Asian, other Asian, Latina, and Black women can develop diabetes at a lower body weight, so screening may start earlier — for Asian American women, at a body mass index (BMI) of 23 or more. If diabetes runs in your family, tell your doctor.

Cancer screenings: cervical, breast, and colon

Cervical cancer (Pap and HPV test). The Pap smear (a gentle swab that collects cells from the cervix) and the HPV test (which looks for a common virus that can lead to cervical cancer) find changes early, when they are easy to treat. The USPSTF recommends:

  • Ages 21 to 29: a Pap test every 3 years.
  • Ages 30 to 65: a Pap test every 3 years, OR an HPV test every 5 years, OR both together every 5 years.

Screening usually is not needed before 21, or after 65 if your past tests were normal (USPSTF, cervical cancer screening).

Breast cancer (mammogram). A mammogram is a low-dose X-ray of the breast. In 2024, the USPSTF updated its advice: women should start getting a mammogram at age 40, and repeat it every 2 years through age 74 (USPSTF, breast cancer screening). This is a change from the old advice to wait until 50. Starting at 40 helps find cancer earlier. If you have a strong family history of breast cancer, ask your doctor whether you should start sooner.

Colon and rectal cancer (colorectal screening). This cancer is very treatable when found early, and screening can even prevent it. You can screen with a stool test you do at home, or a colonoscopy (a camera exam of the colon). The USPSTF recommends starting at age 45 and continuing to age 75 (USPSTF, colorectal cancer screening). The starting age used to be 50 — it is now 45. Ask us which test is right for you; the at-home stool test is simple and needs no prep.

Bone health as you get older

Bone density (osteoporosis). As women age, bones can grow thin and break more easily — this is called osteoporosis. A bone density scan (also called a DEXA scan) is a quick, painless X-ray that checks bone strength. The USPSTF recommends this scan for all women age 65 and older. Younger women who have gone through menopause and have risk factors — such as a low body weight, smoking, or a parent who broke a hip — may need it earlier (USPSTF, osteoporosis screening).

Mental health, sexual health, and vaccines

Mental health. Your mind matters as much as your body. The USPSTF recommends that all adults, including pregnant and new mothers, be screened for depression (USPSTF, depression screening). This is often just a few questions on paper or on a tablet. If you have felt sad, worried, or hopeless, please tell us. Help is available, and it works.

Sexually transmitted infections (STIs). Some infections have no signs but can be treated easily. The USPSTF recommends chlamydia and gonorrhea testing for all sexually active women age 24 and younger, and for older women who have a higher risk (USPSTF, chlamydia and gonorrhea screening). Testing is private and simple, often a urine sample.

Vaccines. Vaccines are a form of prevention, too. The HPV vaccine protects against the virus that causes most cervical cancers. The CDC recommends it for preteens at ages 11 to 12, with catch-up doses through age 26 if you missed it. Some adults age 27 to 45 may still choose it after talking with their doctor (CDC, HPV vaccine). The CDC also recommends a flu shot every year for everyone age 6 months and older, plus staying up to date on the COVID-19 vaccine (CDC, who needs a flu vaccine).

Key takeaways

  • Blood pressure: check yearly at age 40+ (all adults age 18+ should be checked).
  • Diabetes (A1c): age 35 to 70 if overweight — often earlier for South Asian, Asian, and Latina women.
  • Cervical cancer: Pap every 3 years from age 21; add or switch to HPV testing every 5 years from age 30.
  • Breast cancer: mammogram starting at age 40, every 2 years.
  • Colon cancer: start screening at age 45 — an at-home stool test is an easy option.
  • Bone density: DEXA scan for all women at age 65 (earlier if you have risk factors).
  • Also ask about mental health, STI testing, and vaccines (HPV, flu, COVID-19).

These dates are general guides. Your own plan may differ based on your health and family history. A yearly checkup — sometimes called a well-woman visit — is the easiest way to stay on track. See our women's health & preventive screening services, or book a well-woman visit in your language.

Questions to ask your doctor

  • Based on my age and history, which screenings do I need this year?
  • Should I start my mammogram or diabetes test earlier because of my family history?
  • For my colon check, is the at-home stool test a good option for me?
  • Are these screenings covered by my insurance, and what will they cost?
  • Which vaccines am I due for — including HPV, flu, and COVID-19?
  • Can I do more than one screening at the same visit to save time?

Trusted resources & sources

  • U.S. Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) — the expert panel behind most screening ages and intervals above.
  • CDC — HPV vaccine and CDC — flu vaccine.
  • Office on Women's Health (womenshealth.gov) — general women's health guides. A full Spanish site is at espanol.womenshealth.gov.
  • MedlinePlus — health screenings for women, from the National Institutes of Health. Most MedlinePlus pages offer a Spanish version (look for the "Español" link).
  • Detailed USPSTF pages: breast, cervical, colorectal, and osteoporosis.
