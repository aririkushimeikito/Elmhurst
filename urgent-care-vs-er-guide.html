Urgent Care or ER? A Symptom-by-Symptom Guide

Urgent care or emergency room? A symptom-by-symptom guide from our Elmhurst, Queens clinic — plus real cost and wait-time data and when to call 911 instead.

By Keerthana Rajagopal, PA, Elmhurst Medical · Medically reviewed by Dr. Ferdous Salauddin, MD, Internal Medicine · Updated August 2026

It is 6pm, something hurts, and you are standing in your kitchen trying to decide: urgent care or emergency room? Guess wrong toward the ER and you may lose five hours and a few thousand dollars. Guess wrong the other way and you lose time that actually matters.

Here is the honest version of that decision — including the symptoms where you should stop reading and call 911.

What is the actual decision rule?

There is one, and it is short.

If the problem could kill you, disable you permanently, or cost you an organ or a limb — that is the ER, and if it is happening fast, that is 911. Everything that is not that, but also cannot wait for a routine appointment, is urgent care.

The National Library of Medicine puts it in almost exactly those terms: if a person or an unborn baby could die or be permanently disabled, it is an emergency; urgent care is for problems that are not life threatening and not risking disability, but that you cannot get seen for soon enough (MedlinePlus, NIH).

Three rules that override everything else in this article:

  1. Call 911 for anything sudden and severe. Do not drive yourself. Do not have a relative drive you. An ambulance starts treatment on the way, and patients with chest pain who arrive by ambulance often get treated faster (American Heart Association).
  2. Cost is never a reason to skip the ER in an emergency. Under federal law (EMTALA), any hospital that participates in Medicare and offers emergency services must screen you and stabilize an emergency condition regardless of your insurance or ability to pay (CMS).
  3. When in doubt, treat it as an emergency. Being wrong in that direction costs money. Being wrong in the other direction can cost much more.

What can urgent care actually do — and not do?

This is where most confusion lives. Urgent care is not a small ER; it is a well-equipped clinic.

Usually yes:

  • X-ray for chest, head, body, and extremities
  • Stitches, wound care, splinting, and minor procedures
  • Blood counts, urine tests, strep, flu and COVID testing, EKG
  • IV fluids and IV medications in many clinics
  • Prescriptions on the spot

The American Academy of Urgent Care Medicine's own equipment list includes X-ray, hematology equipment, EKG, microscope, centrifuge, and IVs — though it varies by clinic, so it is worth a phone call before you go (Zocdoc guide to AAUCM standards).

No, and this is the important part:

  • CT scans and MRI — so no ruling out a brain bleed, a pulmonary embolism, or appendicitis by imaging
  • Surgery of any kind
  • Admitting you to a hospital bed or monitoring you overnight
  • Blood transfusion, ventilators, cardiac catheterization
  • Trauma care

If what you need is any item on that second list, urgent care will end up sending you to the ER anyway — which costs you a whole extra visit.

What is the real difference in cost and wait time?

Cost first. Across people with employer-sponsored insurance in 2022, the average price of an urgent care visit was $220, compared with $2,256 for an emergency room visit (Health Care Cost Institute). Same-diagnosis comparisons are starker: a urinary tract infection cost $218 at urgent care versus $2,511 in the ER — and the average out-of-pocket share was $83 versus $545.

Independent research lines up. A Health Affairs study found that in 2019 the average lower-acuity ED visit cost $1,716 versus $178 at an urgent care center — about 9.6 times more (Health Affairs, 2021). An analysis of ten common primary-care-treatable conditions put the hospital ED average at $2,032, versus $193 at urgent care and $167 at a doctor's office (UnitedHealth Group).

Now time. Nationally, the median wait just to see an ED clinician was 16 minutes and the mean was 38.1 minutes in 2022 (CDC/NCHS National Hospital Ambulatory Medical Care Survey) — but that is only the wait to be seen. Median total time in the ED has run around 180 minutes, including testing and treatment (CDC data via ACEP). And the sicker the patients ahead of you, the longer you wait, because triage is by severity, not arrival order.

What about kids?

Children are not small adults, and the thresholds are different.

  • Under 3 months old: a rectal temperature of 100.4°F (38°C) or higher means call the pediatrician right away — this age group needs urgent evaluation because their immune systems are immature (AAP).
  • Any age: call right away for a temperature repeatedly above 104°F (40°C), or for a stiff neck, severe headache, breathing difficulty, unexplained rash, repeated vomiting or diarrhea, a seizure, or a child who looks very ill, unusually drowsy, or is inconsolable (AAP).
  • Under 2 years: also call if fever lasts more than 24 hours. Age 2 and up: more than 3 days.
  • Trust behavior over the thermometer. A child with 103°F who is drinking and playing worries us less than a child with 101°F who will not wake up properly.
  • Serious pediatric trauma, and any child who needs sedation or a CT, belongs in a hospital with pediatric emergency capability — not urgent care.

When to see a doctor about this

Call 911 now for: chest pain or pressure; sudden trouble breathing; any B.E. F.A.S.T. stroke sign; heavy bleeding that will not stop with pressure; a seizure lasting over a minute; loss of consciousness; a severe allergic reaction; a severe burn; suspected neck or spine injury; or a fever with stiff neck and severe headache.

Come to urgent care — today, not next week — for: fever with cough or sore throat, painful urination, an ear that hurts, a cut that may need stitches, a swollen ankle after a fall, a rash that appeared this week, a mild burn, vomiting you are still managing, a flare of asthma you can still speak through, or a medication you ran out of.

Book a regular primary care visit for: blood pressure that has been creeping up, blood sugar checks, a lump you have had for months, fatigue, weight changes, mood, or the screenings you are due for by age.

And if you truly cannot tell which one you need — call us. Talking to a person for two minutes is cheaper than every other option on this page. If you would rather have that conversation in Bengali, Hindi, Urdu, or Spanish, we can do that too.

Frequently asked questions

Is urgent care or the emergency room cheaper?+

Urgent care, by a wide margin. In 2022 the average urgent care visit price was $220 versus $2,256 for an ER visit, and for identical diagnoses the ER can run ten times higher (Health Care Cost Institute). None of that should factor into a real emergency.

Can urgent care do X-rays and stitches?+

Most can do both. AAUCM standards call for on-site X-ray, lab equipment, EKG, and the ability to do minor procedures such as suturing, but capability varies clinic to clinic, so call first (AAUCM standards summary).

Should I go to urgent care for chest pain?+

No. Call 911. The American Heart Association is unambiguous: call 911 for any heart-attack warning sign, even if you are not certain (AHA). Urgent care has no cardiac catheterization lab.

How long will I wait in the ER?+

The national median wait to see an ED clinician was 16 minutes in 2022, but median total time in the department has been about 3 hours (CDC/NCHS). If your problem is not urgent, you will be triaged behind everyone whose problem is.

Can I go to the ER without insurance?+

Yes. Hospitals participating in Medicare must provide a medical screening exam and stabilize an emergency condition regardless of insurance status or ability to pay (CMS EMTALA). You may still get a bill, so ask about financial assistance.

What if urgent care decides I need the ER after all?+

Then we send you, with our exam findings and any X-rays or labs already done, so the hospital is not starting from zero. It happens, and it is a normal part of the job — not a failure.
