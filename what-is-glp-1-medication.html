What Is a GLP-1 Medication? A Plain-English Guide

What is GLP-1 medication? A doctor in Elmhurst, Queens explains how these drugs work, who qualifies, side effects, cost, and what happens if you stop.

By Dr. Choudhury Hasan, MD, Internal Medicine, Elmhurst Medical · Medically reviewed by Dr. Ferdous Salauddin, MD · Updated August 2026

You have probably heard the names — Ozempic, Wegovy, Mounjaro, Zepbound — from a cousin, a coworker, or an ad on the subway. What you may not have heard is a straight answer: what is GLP-1 medication, who is it really for, and what does it cost you? This is the version of that conversation I have in the exam room, written down.

What is a GLP-1 medication, in plain language?

GLP-1 stands for glucagon-like peptide-1, a hormone your own gut makes after you eat. These medications copy that hormone and act on the parts of your brain that control appetite, which is why people on them feel full sooner and think about food less (National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases).

They also help the body release insulin more appropriately after meals, which is why the same class treats type 2 diabetes. Most are once-weekly injections you give yourself with a small pen needle; one version, Rybelsus, is a daily pill (NIDDK).

Interest has surged. Nationally, the share of adults who had heard "a lot" about these drugs jumped from 19% to 32% in under a year, and about 12% said they had used one (KFF Health Tracking Poll). Prescriptions for people aged 12 to 25 rose 594% between 2020 and 2023 (JAMA).

Ozempic, Wegovy, Mounjaro, Zepbound — what's the difference?

There are only a few actual drugs here. The rest are brand names for the same molecule approved for a different purpose. | Drug (generic) | Brand for weight | Brand for type 2 diabetes | How it works | |---|---|---|---| | Semaglutide | Wegovy (weekly injection) | Ozempic (weekly injection), Rybelsus (daily pill) | Mimics GLP-1 | | Tirzepatide | Zepbound (weekly injection) | Mounjaro (weekly injection) | Mimics GLP-1 and a second hormone, GIP | | Liraglutide | Saxenda (daily injection) | Victoza (daily injection) | Mimics GLP-1 |

Source: NIDDK.

So Ozempic and Wegovy are the same medicine at different approved doses for different official uses — a distinction that matters enormously to your insurance company, and often the real reason one gets covered and the other does not.

Who qualifies for a GLP-1?

For the weight-management versions, the approved use is alongside a reduced-calorie diet and more physical activity — not instead of them. Wegovy's prescribing information covers adults and patients 12 and older with obesity, and adults with overweight who also have at least one weight-related condition; it is also approved to reduce major cardiovascular events in adults with established heart disease plus obesity or overweight (Wegovy prescribing information, DailyMed).

In the trials, "obesity" meant a BMI of 30 or higher, and "overweight" meant BMI 27 to 29.9 plus a condition such as high blood pressure, high cholesterol, or sleep apnea (DailyMed).

BMI alone is a blunt tool, especially in a neighborhood like ours. The American Diabetes Association recommends diabetes screening at a BMI of 25 or higher — but at 23 or higher in people of Asian ancestry, because South Asian and East Asian adults develop type 2 diabetes at lower body weights — and lists Latino, African American, Native American, and Asian American ancestry as risk factors in their own right (ADA Standards of Care in Diabetes—2026). If your number looks "borderline," that is a reason to be evaluated, not dismissed.

Some people should not take these medicines. The labels carry a boxed warning about thyroid C-cell tumors seen in rodents, and they are not for anyone with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma or the genetic syndrome MEN 2 (DailyMed). They are also not for use in pregnancy, while trying to conceive, or while breastfeeding (NIDDK).

What results are realistic?

Honest numbers, from published trials rather than advertising:

  • In the 68-week STEP 1 trial, adults on weekly semaglutide 2.4 mg lost an average of 14.9% of body weight, versus 2.4% on placebo (New England Journal of Medicine).
  • In the 72-week SURMOUNT-1 trial of tirzepatide, average reduction was 16.0% to 22.5% depending on dose, versus 2.4% on placebo (Eli Lilly).
  • Across obesity medications generally, people on a medication plus a lifestyle program lose about 3% to 12% more of their starting weight over a year than with lifestyle changes alone, and most of that happens in the first six months (NIDDK).

An average is not a promise. Some people lose much more, some much less, and no one can predict which you will be. Worth knowing: losing even 5% to 10% of body weight tends to improve blood sugar, blood pressure, triglycerides, joint pain, and sleep apnea (NIDDK) — for many of my patients with type 2 diabetes or high blood pressure, that is the real goal.

What are the common side effects?

Most side effects are digestive, appear while the dose is being increased, and settle down. In STEP 1: nausea 44%, diarrhea 30%, vomiting 24%, constipation 24%, abdominal pain 20%, headache 14%, fatigue 11% — most gastrointestinal events mild to moderate and temporary (NEJM; DailyMed).

More serious issues did occur: about 7% stopped because of side effects, gallbladder problems affected 2.6%, and acute pancreatitis 0.2% (NEJM). Some people taking obesity medications have reported suicidal thoughts and the FDA is still evaluating whether the drugs are responsible — tell your clinician right away if this happens (NIDDK).

What does insurance actually cover — and what if it doesn't?

List prices in the US run roughly $936 to $1,349 per month before insurance (KFF). That is why coverage is the whole ballgame.

  • Among large employers (200+ workers), 19% cover GLP-1s primarily for weight loss, rising to 43% at firms with 5,000+ workers; about a third of covering employers require you to join a lifestyle or dietitian program (KFF 2025 Employer Health Benefits Survey).
  • Medicare Part D is barred by law from covering drugs used for weight loss, but does cover them for type 2 diabetes and approved cardiovascular uses (Congressional Research Service).
  • Even insured users struggle: 54% said the medication was somewhat or very difficult to afford (KFF).

If your plan says no, there are legitimate paths: prior authorization with documentation, a covered alternative in the class, manufacturer savings or self-pay programs, or a different treatment plan altogether. I would rather build you a plan that works than watch you buy something unsafe.

Why "cheap online semaglutide" is a real risk

The FDA warns that compounded and unapproved versions are not reviewed for safety, effectiveness, or quality. As of May 31, 2026, the agency had received 990 adverse-event reports involving compounded semaglutide and more than 730 involving compounded tirzepatide, including hospitalizations from dosing errors where the wrong amount was measured (FDA).

Warning signs the FDA lists: prices that seem too good to be true, no screening by a licensed clinician, misspellings on the label, and product that arrives warm. Counterfeit Ozempic has been found in the US supply chain. Buy only from a state-licensed pharmacy (FDA).

What happens when you stop?

This is the question people ask last and should ask first. In the STEP 1 extension study, participants who stopped at week 68 regained an average of 11.6 percentage points of body weight by week 120 — roughly two-thirds of what they had lost. They were still down 5.6% from baseline, and 48% had kept at least a 5% loss (Diabetes, Obesity and Metabolism).

That is not a failure of willpower. Obesity behaves like a chronic condition, and most people regain some weight when treatment stops (NIDDK). So discuss the plan on day one: how long, what happens if coverage changes, and what maintenance looks like.

Why does medical supervision matter?

A prescription is the smallest part of this. Dose increases need pacing to your symptoms, thyroid and pancreatic history reviewed, other medications — insulin and sulfonylureas especially — often adjusted, and blood work rechecked. Nationally, 79% of users got their prescription from a primary care doctor or specialist, 11% from an online-only provider and 10% from a med spa (KFF). The difference is follow-up: someone should be asking how you are actually eating and whether your numbers are moving, not just refilling a pen.

When to see a doctor about this

Get evaluated before starting, and get seen promptly if you are on a GLP-1 and notice:

  • Severe or constant abdominal pain, especially spreading to your back — possible pancreatitis or gallbladder disease (NEJM)
  • Vomiting or diarrhea you cannot keep ahead of, or dehydration signs like dizziness on standing and very little urine
  • A lump or swelling in your neck, hoarseness, or trouble swallowing (DailyMed)
  • Shakiness, sweating, or confusion suggesting low blood sugar, especially if you also take insulin or a sulfonylurea
  • New or worsening thoughts of harming yourself, or a positive pregnancy test or plans to conceive (NIDDK)

Chest pain or pressure, trouble breathing, fainting, or sudden weakness or trouble speaking are 911 situations (CDC).

Frequently asked questions

Is Ozempic the same thing as Wegovy?+

Both are semaglutide from the same company, but they are approved for different uses and dosed differently — Ozempic for type 2 diabetes, Wegovy for weight management and cardiovascular risk reduction (NIDDK). Insurance often treats them very differently for that reason.

Do I have to take a GLP-1 forever?+

Not necessarily, but plan for what comes after. Most people regain a meaningful share of lost weight after stopping, so we discuss maintenance or alternatives rather than an abrupt stop (Diabetes, Obesity and Metabolism).

Will my insurance cover it for weight loss?+

Often not, and it depends heavily on your plan. Only 19% of larger employers cover these drugs primarily for weight loss, and Medicare Part D cannot cover weight-loss use at all (KFF; CRS). We check your benefits before you commit to anything.

Are compounded or online GLP-1s safe?+

The FDA has documented dosing errors, hospitalizations, and counterfeit product tied to unapproved sources (FDA). If you are already using one, bring the vial and label to your visit — no lecture, we just need to know what you are taking.
