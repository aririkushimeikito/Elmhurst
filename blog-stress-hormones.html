How Stress Affects Your Hormones — and Your Health

Stress is not "just in your head." When worry never lets up, your body keeps pumping out a hormone called cortisol — and over time that can touch your blood sugar, your weight, your sleep, and your heart. Here is what is really happening, and what helps.

You feel it before you can name it. Your chest tightens, your jaw clenches, your mind races at 2 a.m. It is easy to think stress is only a feeling. But stress is also a full-body event. When your brain senses pressure — a stack of bills, a hard shift, a sick parent, a new country to figure out — it sets off a chain of hormones (chemical messengers that travel in your blood) that change how your whole body works.

In short bursts, this system is a gift. It helped your ancestors run from danger and it helps you today when you need to act fast. The problem for many of us in Elmhurst and across Queens is that the stress rarely switches off. When it stays on for weeks and months, those same helpful hormones start to wear the body down. Understanding this can help you take it seriously — and take back some control.

Among U.S. adults ages 35–44, the share reporting a chronic illness rose to 58% in 2023, up from 48% in 2019 — a jump the survey ties to years of high stress. Source: American Psychological Association, Stress in America 2023

Your body's built-in alarm system

When your brain reads a situation as a threat, it flips on a pathway doctors call the HPA axis. That is a chain of three parts talking to each other: the hypothalamus and pituitary in your brain, and the adrenal glands, two small glands that sit on top of your kidneys. In seconds, that chain tells your adrenal glands to release two key stress hormones. Cleveland Clinic

  • Adrenaline (also called epinephrine) is the fast one. It speeds your heart, quickens your breathing, and sends blood to your big muscles so you are ready to move. Endocrine Society
  • Cortisol is the main stress hormone. It is slower and steadier. It raises your blood sugar to give you quick fuel, sharpens your focus, and quiets systems your body decides can wait — like digestion and reproduction. Endocrine Society

This is the "fight-or-flight" response. Your blood pressure, heart rate, and blood sugar all climb so you can act. MedlinePlus When the pressure passes, cortisol is supposed to signal the alarm to shut off, and your body settles back to normal.

Short bursts help. Stress that never stops hurts.

A rush of stress hormones is useful when you are giving a big presentation, playing a sport, or reacting to real danger. NIH News in Health The trouble starts when the "off" switch never gets pressed. If money worries, long hours, family duties, or hard memories keep the alarm ringing, your body stays flooded with cortisol day after day. That is called chronic stress, and it is a very different thing from a stressful afternoon.

Health experts link long-term stress to higher levels of inflammation (irritation inside the body) that is thought to feed many diseases as we age. NIH News in Health Over time, that steady strain can affect your immune system, your digestion, your heart and blood vessels, and your reproductive system, and it raises the risk of anxiety and depression. National Institute of Mental Health

What ongoing high cortisol can do

Cortisol touches almost every organ, so nonstop stress can show up in many ways. Here are the ones that matter most for the families we care for.

  • It raises blood sugar. Cortisol's job is to push sugar into your blood for quick energy. When cortisol stays high, blood sugar can stay high too — a real problem for anyone who has diabetes or is at risk for it. Endocrine Society This matters a great deal in South Asian and Latino families, where diabetes is common. See our guide on managing diabetes and everyday care.
  • It encourages belly fat and cravings. Researchers found that on stressful days, people burned fewer calories after a fatty meal and made more insulin, a hormone that helps store fat. NIH News in Health High cortisol also nudges you toward sugary, fatty comfort foods, and tends to park extra weight around the middle.
  • It steals your sleep. Cortisol is meant to fall at night so you can rest. When stress keeps it high, you lie awake or wake too early. Then poor sleep raises stress hormones the next day — a loop that feeds itself. Sleep problems are one of the most common signs of too much stress. NIMH
  • It disrupts periods and fertility. When cortisol is high, the body turns down the reproductive system because it treats survival as more urgent than having a baby. Endocrine Society Ongoing stress can make periods late, irregular, or absent and can interfere with ovulation (releasing an egg), which makes it harder to get pregnant. Chronic stress and ovulatory dysfunction, NIH/PMC
  • It can lower testosterone in men. The same "not now" signal that quiets reproduction can blunt testosterone, the main male sex hormone. High cortisol appears to block testosterone's effects, which over time can dampen energy, mood, and sex drive. University of Texas at Austin
  • It strains your blood pressure and heart. Stress hormones raise blood pressure and heart rate again and again. Over years, that wear-and-tear is linked to high blood pressure and heart disease. MedlinePlus
  • It can worsen thyroid symptoms, anxiety, and depression. Because cortisol reaches the brain and other glands, heavy stress can make thyroid problems feel worse and raises the risk of anxiety disorders and depression. NIMH

If several of these sound like you, please hear this clearly: it is not a personal failing, and you are not imagining it. Your body is doing exactly what it was built to do — just for too long.

What actually helps

The hopeful part is that cortisol responds to how you live. You do not need to erase stress (no one can). You need to give your body regular chances to switch the alarm off. Small, steady steps work better than big, short-lived ones.

  • Protect your sleep. A steady bedtime, a dark cool room, and less caffeine and screen time at night help cortisol fall when it should. NIMH lists a regular sleep schedule and cutting back on caffeine among its top coping tips. NIMH
  • Move your body. Regular activity — even brisk walking — helps lower stress hormones and lifts your mood. It does not have to be a gym; a daily walk after dinner counts. CDC
  • Build in recovery, boundaries, and support. Slow breathing, prayer, time in nature, and leaning on people who care for you all calm the stress response. Reaching out to friends or family who help you cope is one of NIMH's key recommendations. NIMH
  • Get checked. Ask your doctor to look at your blood sugar, blood pressure, and — if it fits your symptoms — your thyroid. These simple tests can catch the physical footprints of stress early, while they are easy to treat.
  • Ask for mental-health support. If stress or anxiety will not ease or is getting in the way of your life, talk therapy and, when needed, medication both help. NIMH This is ordinary medical care, not something to feel ashamed of.

Key takeaways

  • Stress is physical: your brain triggers the HPA axis, which releases adrenaline and cortisol, the main stress hormone.
  • In short bursts these hormones help. Nonstop stress keeps cortisol high, which wears the body down.
  • Long-term high cortisol can raise blood sugar, add belly fat and cravings, harm sleep, disrupt periods and fertility, lower testosterone, strain the heart, and worsen thyroid symptoms, anxiety, and depression.
  • What helps: protect sleep, move your body, build in recovery and support, and get your blood sugar, blood pressure, and thyroid checked.
  • Ongoing stress or anxiety is treatable — talk therapy and medication both work. Asking for help is smart, not weak.

Questions to ask your doctor

  • Could my symptoms — poor sleep, weight gain, irregular periods, low energy — be tied to stress and its effect on my hormones?
  • Should I have my blood sugar, blood pressure, or thyroid checked?
  • I already have diabetes or high blood pressure — how might stress be affecting my numbers?
  • What are safe, realistic ways for me to lower stress with my work and family schedule?
  • Would talking to a counselor or therapist help me, and can you connect me to one who speaks my language?
  • Are any of my current medicines or habits making my stress or sleep worse?
