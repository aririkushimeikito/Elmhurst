Hormonal Imbalance: Common Causes and Signs

"Hormonal imbalance" is a common phrase, but it is not one single problem. It is a sign that something behind the scenes — often very treatable — is off. Here is what it means and what usually causes it.

Hormones are your body's chemical messengers. They travel in your blood and help control your energy, weight, mood, sleep, sex drive, and monthly cycle. Because even a tiny amount of a hormone can create big changes, having too much or too little of one can affect how you feel all over (MedlinePlus). When people say "hormonal imbalance," this is usually what they mean — one or more hormones running too high or too low.

The good news: a hormonal imbalance is almost never a mystery you have to live with. It is a signal. Most of the time, a simple conversation and a few blood tests can point to the real cause — and most causes are very treatable.

The wide range of signs

Hormone problems can look very different from person to person. That is part of why they are missed for so long. Common signs include:

  • Feeling tired all the time, even after a full night of sleep
  • Weight gain or weight loss that you cannot explain
  • Mood changes — feeling low, anxious, or short-tempered
  • Irregular, missing, or very heavy periods
  • Low sex drive (low libido)
  • Changes in your hair (thinning or extra hair) or dry, changing skin
  • Trouble falling asleep or staying asleep
  • Feeling too hot or too cold when others feel fine

One or two of these on their own may mean nothing. But several together, or a change that does not go away, is worth checking. Below are the most common reasons behind these signs.

Thyroid problems

Your thyroid is a small, butterfly-shaped gland in the front of your neck. It makes hormones that set your body's "speed." These hormones affect your heart rate, weight, digestion, and mood (MedlinePlus). When the thyroid makes too little (hypothyroidism), people often feel tired, cold, and gain weight. When it makes too much (hyperthyroidism), people may feel jittery, lose weight, and have a racing heart. Thyroid problems are common — especially in women — and are usually found with one simple blood test.

PCOS and insulin resistance

PCOS (polycystic ovary syndrome) is one of the most common hormone conditions in women of childbearing age. It can cause irregular periods, extra hair growth, acne, and trouble getting pregnant. Many women with PCOS also have insulin resistance — this is when the body makes insulin (the hormone that controls blood sugar) but cannot use it well. This raises the risk of type 2 diabetes (CDC).

More than 1 in 2 — More than half of women with PCOS develop type 2 diabetes by age 40, which is why early testing matters. Source: CDC

Perimenopause and menopause

Menopause is the natural stage when monthly periods stop for good. The years of change leading up to it are called perimenopause. During this time, the ovaries make much less estrogen and progesterone (National Institute on Aging). Those shifting hormones can bring hot flashes, night sweats, irregular periods, trouble sleeping, mood changes, and vaginal dryness. This is a normal part of aging, not a disease — but if symptoms are making daily life hard, there are safe ways to feel better.

Age 52 — The average age of menopause in the United States, though the change often begins years earlier, between ages 45 and 55. Source: National Institute on Aging

Diabetes and prediabetes

Insulin is a hormone, so diabetes is, at its heart, a hormone problem. In prediabetes and type 2 diabetes, the body cannot keep blood sugar in a healthy range. Early on there may be no clear signs at all, which is why so many people do not know they have it. Testing is simple and important.

8 in 10 — About 8 in 10 U.S. adults with prediabetes do not know they have it — a condition that a simple blood test can catch early. Source: CDC

This matters for our neighbors in Queens. Type 2 diabetes runs higher in many South Asian, East Asian, and Latino families, so if diabetes is in your family, it is worth asking your doctor about a blood sugar check even if you feel fine.

Chronic stress and poor sleep

When you are under stress, your body releases cortisol (the body's main stress hormone). A short burst is healthy and helps you handle a tough moment. But when stress lasts a long time, it can begin to harm your health (NIH News in Health). Long-term stress can raise body-wide inflammation, change how your body handles weight, worsen your mood, and disrupt your sleep. And here is the tricky part: poor sleep and high stress feed each other, keeping other hormones off balance too. Caring for your sleep and stress is not a luxury — it is part of hormone health.

Low testosterone in men

Hormone shifts are not only a women's issue. In men, testosterone naturally drops with age, and in some men it drops enough to cause symptoms — a condition called hypogonadism (low testosterone). Common signs include a drop in sex drive, low energy, loss of muscle, and a low or irritable mood (Endocrine Society). It is more common than many men expect, and it is diagnosed with early-morning blood tests, not guesswork.

About 35% — Roughly 35% of men older than 45 have low testosterone, and it is even more common in men with obesity or type 2 diabetes. Source: Endocrine Society

Certain medications

Sometimes the cause is a medicine you are already taking. Steroids (like prednisone), some medicines for pain, certain birth control and hormone treatments, and a few medicines for mood or blood pressure can shift your hormone levels. This does not mean you should stop them — many are important. It means you should tell your doctor about every medicine and supplement you take, so the full picture makes sense.

When to see a doctor — and why a simple blood test helps

Consider making an appointment if you notice symptoms that last more than a few weeks, periods that become very irregular or stop, unexplained weight change, ongoing tiredness or low mood, a low sex drive, or hair and skin changes that bother you. You do not need to have "figured it out" before you come in.

The reassuring part is that most of these causes can be found with basic, low-cost blood tests — for thyroid hormones, blood sugar, and, when it fits, estrogen or testosterone. Key takeaways

  • "Hormonal imbalance" is not one disease — it is a sign that a hormone is running too high or too low.
  • The most common causes are thyroid problems, PCOS with insulin resistance, perimenopause and menopause, diabetes and prediabetes, chronic stress and poor sleep, low testosterone in men, and certain medications.
  • Signs vary widely: fatigue, weight change, mood shifts, irregular periods, low sex drive, hair and skin changes, and sleep trouble.
  • Many hormone problems have no early symptoms, so testing matters even when you feel okay.
  • Simple, affordable blood tests can usually find the cause.
  • Almost every cause is treatable — you do not have to just live with feeling off.

Questions to ask your doctor

  • Could my symptoms be from my thyroid, blood sugar, or another hormone?
  • Which blood tests do I need, and do I need to fast or come in the morning?
  • Could any of my current medicines be affecting my hormones?
  • My family has diabetes or thyroid problems — should I be tested sooner?
  • What can I change at home — sleep, stress, food, movement — that would help?
  • When should I come back to recheck my levels?
