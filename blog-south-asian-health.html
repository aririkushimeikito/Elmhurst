Why South Asians Face Higher Heart & Diabetes Risk

If your family comes from South Asia, your heart and blood sugar may need attention earlier than you think — even if you feel fine and the scale looks normal. Here is why, and what you can do about it.

Walk down Broadway in Elmhurst and you will hear Bengali, Hindi, Urdu, Nepali, and more. Our families work hard, cook with love, and care deeply for one another. But there is a health truth that too few of us hear in time: people with roots in South Asia — India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Nepal, Sri Lanka — tend to get heart disease and type 2 diabetes (high blood sugar) more often, and at younger ages, than many other groups. And it can happen even when you are not overweight.

This is not meant to scare you. It is meant to help you and the people you love act early, while prevention works best. The science is clear, and the steps that protect you are simple and doable.

Around 60% of the world's heart disease patients have South Asian roots, even though South Asians are only about a quarter of the world's people. Source: MASALA Study, UCSF

The risk starts earlier — and runs higher

Large studies show the heart risk for South Asians is real and measurable. In a study of more than 450,000 people in the United Kingdom, people of South Asian ancestry had more than double the risk of developing heart disease compared with people of European ancestry. Even after doctors accounted for the usual risk factors — blood pressure, cholesterol, smoking, diabetes — the South Asian group still had about a 45% higher risk that the usual factors could not explain. American Heart Association

Diabetes follows the same pattern. In the MASALA study — a long-running study of South Asians living in America, based at UCSF and Northwestern — about 1 in 4 South Asian adults already had type 2 diabetes, and another 1 in 3 had prediabetes (blood sugar that is higher than normal but not yet diabetes). Heart disease in our community also tends to strike earlier, often before age 50, and can be more aggressive. American College of Cardiology

Because the risk is real, major heart groups now treat South Asian background itself as a "risk-enhancing factor." That means your doctor should count your heritage as a reason to look more closely and act sooner — not wait. The American Heart Association published a full scientific statement in its journal Circulation laying out this higher and earlier risk. AHA Scientific Statement, Circulation

"Thin outside, fat inside": why the scale can fool you

Here is the part that surprises many families. You can look slim, wear the same clothing size for years, and still carry hidden risk. Researchers sometimes call this "thin outside, fat inside." The MASALA study found that, compared with other groups, South Asians tend to carry more visceral fat (fat stored deep around the organs, like the liver) and more fat inside the liver itself — while having less muscle. This kind of hidden fat is closely tied to heart disease and diabetes. MASALA Study coverage, The Scientist

What does this mean for you day to day? It means the bathroom scale is not the whole story. Two things often matter more:

  • Your waist. Fat around the middle is a warning sign, even at a "normal" weight. A growing waistline can signal rising visceral fat before your weight changes much.
  • Your blood tests. Numbers like blood sugar, A1c (a 3-month blood-sugar average), cholesterol, and blood pressure can turn unhealthy while you still feel perfectly well.

Because South Asians develop these problems at a lower body weight, the usual cutoffs can miss us. That is why the American Diabetes Association recommends that Asian Americans get screened for diabetes starting at a body-mass index (BMI, a weight-for-height number) of 23 — lower than the 25 used for the general population. Sadly, more than half of Asian Americans with diabetes do not yet know they have it. ADA guidance via diaTribe

Two more things worth knowing

Lipoprotein(a). Many South Asians carry higher levels of a cholesterol particle called lipoprotein(a), or Lp(a). It is largely set by your genes, and high levels raise heart risk on their own. A simple blood test — usually just once in your life — can tell you your number. American College of Cardiology

Family history. If a parent, brother, or sister had a heart attack or diabetes — especially at a young age — your own risk goes up. Knowing your family's health story is one of the most powerful, and free, tools you have. Talk with relatives here and back home, and share what you learn with your doctor.

Prevention that truly saves lives

The best news is that so much of this risk is in your hands. You do not need to wait for symptoms — in fact, waiting is the trap. Chest pain or a diabetes diagnosis is often the late sign, not the early one. Here is where to focus.

  • Start screening earlier. Do not assume checks are only for older people. Ask about your blood pressure, cholesterol, and blood sugar or A1c even in your 30s — sooner if you have a family history. These are quick, low-cost tests.
  • Watch your waist, not just the scale. Notice if your waistband is getting tighter over the months, even when your weight looks steady. Bring it up at your visit.
  • Move most days. Aim for about 30 minutes of activity a day — a brisk walk counts. Movement builds muscle and burns the hidden fat that raises risk. Little bits add up: walk after dinner, take the stairs, get off the train a stop early.
  • Build meals around vegetables, protein, and fiber. Keep the foods you love, and adjust the balance. Fill half your plate with vegetables and dal (lentils), add protein like fish, chicken, eggs, paneer, or beans, and enjoy rice and roti in mindful portions. Choosing brown rice or adding more vegetables to biryani are small, realistic swaps — no need to give up your kitchen's flavor.
  • Know your numbers and your family history. Write down your blood pressure, A1c, cholesterol, and Lp(a). Bring the list to every visit so you can watch the trend.
  • Do not wait for symptoms. Prevention works best before you feel anything is wrong.

None of this means shame or fear. It means a small amount of attention now can protect many years later. Many people in our community lower their risk with modest, steady changes and regular check-ins — no dramatic diet, no giving up the foods that connect you to home.

Key takeaways

  • South Asians tend to develop heart disease and type 2 diabetes more often, and at younger ages, than many other groups.
  • Risk can be high even at a normal weight — "thin outside, fat inside" means more hidden fat around the organs.
  • Your waist size and blood tests often matter more than the number on the scale.
  • Screening should start earlier: Asian Americans are advised to check for diabetes at a lower BMI (23), and heart checks can begin in your 30s.
  • Ask about lipoprotein(a), a mostly-genetic cholesterol test, and learn your family history.
  • Move most days, build meals around vegetables, protein, and fiber with mindful rice and roti — and do not wait for symptoms.

Questions to ask your doctor

  • Given my South Asian background, when should I start checking my blood pressure, cholesterol, and blood sugar?
  • What are my numbers today, and which ones should I watch most closely?
  • Should I have my lipoprotein(a) tested at least once?
  • Is my waist size a concern, even if my weight seems fine?
  • My parent or sibling had heart disease or diabetes — how does that change my plan?
  • What small, realistic changes to my meals and activity would help me most?
