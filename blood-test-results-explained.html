Your Bloodwork, Decoded: The 5 Numbers Every Adult Should Know

What do my blood test results mean? A doctor in Elmhurst, Queens decodes A1C, cholesterol, blood pressure, TSH and vitamin D in plain English.

By Dr. Ferdous Salauddin, MD, Internal Medicine, Elmhurst Medical · Medically reviewed by Dr. Choudhury Hasan, MD · Updated August 2026

Your phone buzzed at 9:40 at night: new lab results in the portal. You saw something flagged in red, and now you are searching what do my blood test results mean while nobody is answering the phone. That is not your doctor ignoring you — results are legally released the moment they are finalized. Here is how to read the five numbers that matter most, and why a single flagged value is almost never an emergency.

First: why did I get these before my doctor called?

Because federal information-blocking rules have required immediate release of test results since April 2021. Most people want it that way — in a survey of 8,139 patients, 95.7% preferred immediate release even if their clinician had not reviewed the result — but 16.5% who received an abnormal result reported increased worry (JAMA Network Open, 2023). If you are anxious right now, you are in a large, well-documented group.

Two facts should take the edge off: one abnormal result does not necessarily mean you have a disease — healthy people sometimes fall outside the reference range — and results are read alongside your exam, history, and often a repeat test, never as a standalone verdict (MedlinePlus, NIH).

1. A1C — your three-month blood sugar average

A1C measures what percentage of your hemoglobin has sugar attached to it, reflecting your average glucose over the past two to three months. No fasting needed. Below 5.7% is normal, 5.7% to 6.4% is prediabetes, and 6.5% or higher is the diabetes range (CDC). Diagnosis normally requires two abnormal results (ADA), so one value of 6.6% is a reason for a second test, not a diagnosis on the spot.

A1C can also be distorted. Anemia, iron deficiency, recent blood loss or transfusion, sickle cell disease, thalassemia, kidney failure, liver disease, pregnancy, and certain medications push it falsely up or down (CDC), and a G6PD variant carried by about 11% of Black Americans lowers A1C by roughly 0.7 to 0.8 percentage points (ADA). 2. Cholesterol panel — LDL, HDL, and triglycerides

A lipid panel gives you several numbers, not one. Optimal levels for adults (CDC, MedlinePlus):

  • Total cholesterol: around 150 mg/dL; above 200 may be considered high
  • LDL ("bad"): about 100 mg/dL or below — the one that deposits plaque in arteries
  • HDL ("good"): at least 40 mg/dL for men, 50 for women; 60 or above is best
  • Triglycerides: under 150 mg/dL
  • Non-HDL cholesterol: under 130 mg/dL

Read them together. An LDL of 130 in a healthy 30-year-old and the same 130 in a 58-year-old with diabetes and high blood pressure lead to completely different conversations. Adults 20 and older are generally checked every five years, then every one to two years for men 45 to 65 and women 55 to 65, and yearly after 65 (MedlinePlus).

3. Blood pressure — the number you can check yourself

Not a blood test, but it belongs here because you can measure it at home for the price of a cuff. Normal is under 120/80; elevated is 120–129 with diastolic under 80; stage 1 hypertension is 130–139 or 80–89; stage 2 is 140 or higher or 90 or higher — and if the two numbers land in different categories you get the higher one (2025 AHA/ACC Guideline). High blood pressure usually has no symptoms at all, so measuring is the only way to know (CDC). And do not let one office reading decide anything: as many as 1 in 3 people with a high office reading have normal readings outside of it (CDC). 4. TSH — your thyroid's signal, not your thyroid

This one confuses almost everyone, because it runs backwards. TSH is made by your pituitary gland, not your thyroid, and it tells the thyroid how much hormone to produce. So a high TSH means your thyroid hormone is too low and a low TSH means there is too much. TSH alone cannot tell you the cause; follow-up may include free T4, T3, and thyroid antibodies for Hashimoto's or Graves' disease (MedlinePlus).

A mildly high TSH with a normal free T4 is subclinical hypothyroidism, and it does not automatically mean medication. People with a TSH above 10 mIU/L are at higher risk of heart failure and cardiovascular death and should be considered for levothyroxine; between the upper limit of normal and 10, treatment is decided individually based on symptoms, antibodies, and heart risk (ATA/AACE Hypothyroidism Guideline) — a position the American Thyroid Association echoes (ATA).

Many mildly elevated TSH values are transient, which is why repeating the test in about three months before treating is common practice (Cleveland Clinic). TSH also runs higher in people over 80 and slightly lower in the first trimester of pregnancy (MedlinePlus). After a dose starts or changes, it is rechecked in four to eight weeks (ATA/AACE).

5. Vitamin D — useful, and widely over-interpreted

The test is 25-hydroxyvitamin D. Per the NIH (Office of Dietary Supplements):

  • Below 12 ng/mL — deficiency, linked to rickets in children and osteomalacia in adults
  • 12 to under 20 ng/mL — generally inadequate
  • 20 ng/mL and above — generally adequate for bone and overall health
  • Above 50 ng/mL — potential for harm, particularly above 60

Recommended intake is 600 IU (15 mcg) daily for adults 19 to 70 and 800 IU over 70. More is not better. Higher-risk groups: darker skin, since melanin reduces vitamin D synthesis; anyone homebound or indoors year-round; people who wear long robes or head coverings; and consistent sunscreen users. UVB does not pass through glass, so a sunny window seat does nothing (NIH).

The honest caveat: the USPSTF issued an "I statement" — insufficient evidence to weigh the benefits and harms of screening asymptomatic adults (USPSTF) — and the Endocrine Society does not recommend routine testing in healthy people (NIH). Test when there is a reason.

Why "normal range" is different on every lab report

Different labs use different testing methods, so they set different reference ranges. Always compare your result to the range printed on your own report, and use the same lab when tracking over time. "Normal" is a statistical range for a reference population, not a personal verdict. Ordinary things shift results too: what you ate or drank, medicines and supplements, hard exercise before the draw, being on your period, and not fasting when told to (MedlinePlus). A slightly odd value after a night shift and a big meal is a reason to repeat the test, not to panic.

When to see a doctor about this

Call 911 or go to an emergency room for chest pain, shortness of breath, one-sided weakness or numbness, trouble speaking, sudden vision change, or a blood pressure over 180/120 with any of those symptoms (AHA).

Get seen within a few days if:

  • Your A1C is 6.5% or higher (ADA)
  • Your TSH is above 10 mIU/L, or is abnormal and you are pregnant or trying to conceive (ATA/AACE)
  • Your vitamin D is below 12 ng/mL (NIH)
  • Triglycerides are very high, or LDL is well above 100 and you have diabetes, high blood pressure, or a family history of early heart disease
  • Home blood pressure readings average 130/80 or higher (2025 AHA/ACC Guideline)
  • You have symptoms that fit the abnormal number — thirst and frequent urination with a high A1C; fatigue, cold intolerance, and weight gain with a high TSH

For a single mildly flagged value with no symptoms, a routine visit is fine — and repeating the test is usually the right next step.

Frequently asked questions

My lab result is flagged red. Do I have a disease?+

Not necessarily. One abnormal result does not mean you have a disease, and healthy people sometimes fall outside the reference range (MedlinePlus). What matters is the size of the abnormality, the trend, and whether it fits your symptoms.

What is a normal TSH level?+

Most adult labs use roughly 0.4 to 4.0 mIU/L, but ranges differ, so use the one printed on your report. High TSH means low thyroid hormone; low TSH means too much (MedlinePlus).

Do I need to fast for these blood tests?+

A1C does not require fasting (CDC). Lipid panels are sometimes drawn fasting and sometimes not — follow the instructions you were given (MedlinePlus).

Should I get vitamin D tested every year?+

Probably not by default. The USPSTF found insufficient evidence to recommend screening asymptomatic adults (USPSTF). If you are in a higher-risk group or have symptoms, testing is reasonable.
